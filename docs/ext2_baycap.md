# Extension 2: Does BayCAP describe the right accuracy?

## Claim

BayCAP's interval describes the accuracy that the classifier has *on average* over all target bags with a given prevalence (the population table $C\operatorname{diag}(\pi^Q)$). The paper scores that interval against the accuracy *of the bag at hand*, which is computed from its realised contingency table (`true_cts`). The two quantities differ, because on a finite bag the classifier is right on a random number of instances. We want to find out whether this mismatch makes BayCAP's intervals miscalibrated on the paper's datasets. If it does, we want to fix it with the *realised-table predictive*, which turns each posterior draw into a plausible table of the bag itself. The fix needs no target labels and no tuning. This matters because a CAP interval is useful only if it covers the quantity it is judged on, and BayCAP is the paper's Bayesian method. E2.1 showed that most of BayCAP's miscalibration is not a too-narrow interval but an interval centred on the wrong value, which the predictive cannot repair; the extension therefore also asks where this displacement comes from. E2.2 found that the largest displacements come from the uniform prior on the columns of $C$, which pulls every recall towards $1/K$; E2.4 tested a prior learned from the validation data instead, which pulls each recall towards the typical recall of the same classifier: it removes the large-$K$ displacement, but harms binary datasets and makes NUTS diverge when its concentrations fall below $1$. A toy simulation traces the binary failures to the fit itself: with two classes the strength of the prior is estimated from a single difference between two recalls, and the plug-in prior ignores the uncertainty of its centre (§T.10); E2.5 tested this on all the binary datasets of `quapy`: the failure is general and is predicted from $V$ alone, since the fit reaches its bound when the two recalls differ by less than about two standard deviations of noise; elsewhere the empirical-Bayes prior is about neutral at $K=2$, and both priors under-cover on datasets with small test pools, consistently with a difference between $V$ and the test pool shared by all bags (cause 4). The user decided on 2026-10-29 that BayCAP may depart from BayesianCC, to be presented as a Bayesian CAP method of its own rather than as BayesianCC applied to CAP; E2.6 therefore screens a hierarchical prior on the recalls, which keeps the typical recall and the spread of the classes unknown instead of plugging in their estimates.

# Experiments

All experiments score every interval against the realised table of each target bag, as `main` does. They use the measures of the paper (AE, coverage, amplitude, Winkler score at $\alpha=0.05$), the $100$ APP bags of size $1000$ of `main` with the same seeds, and target labels only for scoring.

E2.1 estimates, without new runs, how the realised-table predictive would change BayCAP's intervals; E2.2 explains the displacement that E2.1 found; E2.3 runs the exact predictive in `main` and repeats both analyses on it; E2.4 replaces the prior on $C$ that E2.2 found responsible for the largest displacements, with both pushforwards; E2.5 repeats E2.4 on the binary datasets of `quapy` that `main` does not use, to test the explanation of its binary failures (§T.10); E2.6 screens, at no cost, a hierarchical prior on the recalls that would replace the plug-in empirical-Bayes prior (§T.14).

## E2.1: approximate check on stored results

**Why.** In a binary toy simulation (§T.3, toy result), BayCAP's interval covers the population accuracy at the nominal rate, but its coverage of the realised accuracy falls to $0.63$ when the validation set has $5000$ instances per class. The realised-table predictive brings it back to $0.96$. The paper's datasets differ in size, in number of classes and in classifier quality, so the toy says nothing about how large the effect is there. `main` already stores BayCAP's accuracy samples for every pair, so a first answer for accuracy costs no runs.

- **Design.**
  - **Inputs:** stored `main` results for `BayesCAP` at $\lvert U\rvert=1000$ (accuracy samples `estim_acc_ranges`, mean table `estim_cts`, realised table `true_cts`), all $15$ datasets and $5$ classifiers ($75$ pairs).
  - **Varied:** the pushforward, population vs approximate realised-table predictive (§T.4).
  - **Measures:** coverage, amplitude and Winkler score of both intervals, for accuracy only; the ratio $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p$ between the standard deviation the predictive adds and the standard deviation of BayCAP's samples; the median number of validation instances per class $n^s_j=\lvert V\rvert\pi^V_j$.
  - **Estimand:** population table for BayCAP and realised table for the predictive, both scored against the realised table.
  - **Cost:** none, no new runs.
- **Representation.** A per-pair table (rows: dataset, classifier; columns: coverage, amplitude and Winkler score for both intervals, $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p$, median $n^s_j$), produced by `tables/estimand_gap.py` as LaTeX and PDF. A scatter plot is still to be added: BayCAP coverage on the horizontal axis, predictive coverage on the vertical axis, one point per pair, coloured by $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p$, with lines at $0.95$ on both axes. Points that move up from below $0.95$ to the line are pairs the predictive repairs. Points stuck near $(0,0)$ are displaced intervals.
- **Analysis.** First run on 2026-10-14 on the old `BayesCAP` results, generated again on 2026-10-22 on `BayesCAP` recomputed on CPU (`tables/estimand_gap.py`, seed $0$, recorded in §II.10 of `docs/work_plan.md`); the numbers below are those of the CPU results, and differ from the first run only in the count of repaired pairs ($3$ before) and in the largest ratio ($1.50$ before, `dry-bean`):
  - $53$ of the $75$ pairs under-cover (coverage below $0.90$), so BayCAP's intervals miss the realised accuracy much more often than $5\%$ of the time;
  - the approximate predictive brings only $5$ of them within $[0.93,0.97]$ with a lower Winkler score (`spambase` SVM, `mammographic` kNN, `chess` SVM, `nursery` kNN and RF), although it lowers the Winkler score on $56$ pairs;
  - the ratio $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p$ is below $0.3$ on $24$ pairs, where the predictive barely widens the interval, and ranges from $0.07$ (`poker_hand` LR) to $1.78$ (`letter` RF), with about $1.45$–$1.50$ on `dry-bean`;
  - on `isolet` and `letter` ($K=26$) BayCAP's coverage is $0.00$ for every classifier, and the predictive leaves it at $0.00$–$0.02$; on `hand_digits` ($K=10$) BayCAP's coverage is at most $0.10$ and the predictive's at most $0.40$; on these datasets BayCAP's estimate is biased low (LR: $-0.18$ on `isolet`, $-0.07$ on `letter`).

  What this shows: BayCAP under-covers the realised accuracy on most pairs, but in most cases the cause is not an interval that is too narrow around the right value. Widening the interval by the bag-level fluctuation, which is all the predictive can do, rarely repairs coverage. On many pairs, and clearly on the large-$K$ datasets, the interval sits away from the true value (§T.5).

## E2.2: causes of the displacement

**Why.** E2.1 found that BayCAP's interval misses the realised accuracy on most pairs because it is centred on the wrong value, and that widening it does not help. Three candidate causes could produce this, and each predicts a different pattern (§T.5–§T.8): (1) the uniform prior on the columns of $C$ pulls the recalls towards $1/K$, which shows up in the recall term of the error and grows with $K/n^s_j$; (2) an ill-conditioned $C$ amplifies systematic errors into a wrong $\hat\pi$, which shows up in the prevalence term and grows with the condition number; (3) the classifier performs poorly on the problem even on i.i.d. data. All three can be measured on stored data at no cost, and the result tells how much the exact predictive run (E2.3) can be expected to change.

- **Design.**
  - **Inputs:** stored `main` results for `BayesCAP` at $\lvert U\rvert=1000$ (mean table `estim_cts`, realised table `true_cts`, accuracy samples `estim_acc_ranges`), all $75$ pairs; the stored validation posteriors of each classifier (`V_posteriors`) and the validation labels (`validation_y`), from which the validation counts $f_{ij}$ are rebuilt.
  - **Varied:** nothing (analysis). The pairs differ in $K$, in $n^s_j$ and in classifier.
  - **Measures:** vanilla accuracy only. Per bag, then averaged over the $100$ bags of each pair: coverage, amplitude and Winkler score of BayCAP; the shift $\hat A-A$ of the point estimate from the realised accuracy; its recall term and prevalence term (§T.5), which add up exactly to the shift; the prior part of the recall term, $\sum_j\pi_j\left(\frac{f_{jj}+1}{n^s_j+K}-\frac{f_{jj}}{n^s_j}\right)$, computed on the actual $V$; the mean absolute value of the prevalence term, since its sign depends on the bag; the prevalence error $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1$. Per pair: the coverage predicted from the mean shift and the mean amplitude alone (see the analysis); the condition number $\kappa_0(\hat C)$ of the rate matrix of $h$ on $V$, restricted to vectors summing to zero, and the absolute amplification $1/\sigma_{\min}$ on the same vectors, the only one of the two that is informative when $K=2$ (§T.7); the accuracy of $h$ on $V$ (§T.8); $K$, and the minimum and median $n^s_j$.
  - **Estimand:** population table (BayCAP as in `main`), scored against the realised table of each bag. The shift, its terms and the prior part use the bag's true class proportions and recalls, so they are an analysis of the error, not a method.
  - **Cost:** none, no new runs.
- **Representation.**
  - **Table:** one row per (dataset, classifier) pair, grouped by dataset and ordered by $K$, as LaTeX and a landscape PDF like `tables/estimand_gap.py`, so that the diagnostics and the errors of a pair are read side by side. Dataset-level descriptors: $K$, minimum and median $n^s_j$. Three column groups: *diagnostics*, $\kappa_0(\hat C)$, $1/\sigma_{\min}$, accuracy of $h$ on $V$; *interval*, coverage, predicted coverage $\mathcal C_s$, amplitude, Winkler score; *error*, shift, recall term, prior part of the recall term, prevalence term (mean and mean absolute value), $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1$.
  - **Scatter for cause 1:** recall term against its prior part, one point per pair, coloured by $K$, with the line $y=x$; points on the line are pairs whose recall error is the prior's.
  - **Scatter for causes 2 and 3:** $1/\sigma_{\min}$ on the horizontal axis (log scale; pairs with a singular $\hat C$ are counted, not drawn), accuracy of $h$ on $V$ on the vertical axis, one point per pair, marker by classifier; two panels, coloured by coverage and by shift.
  - **How the causes are read:** cause 1 from how much of the recall term its prior part explains; cause 2 from the prevalence term and $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1$ against $1/\sigma_{\min}$ and $\kappa_0(\hat C)$, and from under-coverage ordered along the horizontal axis; cause 3 from under-coverage ordered along the vertical axis instead; comparing the five classifiers within a dataset separates the causes from $K$. The evidence is correlational: the prior is not changed, so effects that also grow with $K/n^s_j$ cannot be ruled out.
- **Analysis.** First run on 2026-10-20 on all $75$ pairs on the old `BayesCAP` results; the table was generated again on 2026-10-22 on `BayesCAP` recomputed on CPU, with the predicted coverage $\mathcal C_s$ (`tables/displacement.py`, recorded in §II.11 of `docs/work_plan.md`), and the numbers below are those of the CPU results, in `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/tables/displacement/displacement.csv`. The two scatters were drawn again on the CPU results on 2026-10-23 (`plots/displacement.py`, commit `975f2ac`); the CPU results change the quoted numbers by at most a few hundredths and do not change any conclusion. Notation: $R$ recall term, $R_0$ its prior part, $P$ prevalence term; $R-R_0$, the part of the recall term that the prior does not explain, and $s=\text{shift}/(\text{amplitude}/2)$, the shift measured in half-widths of the interval, are differences and ratios of columns of the per-pair CSV.
  - **Coverage is a matter of displacement.** The predicted coverage $\mathcal C_s$ answers the question: what coverage would BayCAP have if its only fault were the shift? It is obtained in four steps.
    1. *What a correct interval looks like.* Across the bags of a pair, the error $\hat A-A$ of the point estimate varies. If BayCAP described its uncertainty correctly, this error would follow a bell curve (a normal distribution) centred at $0$ with some standard deviation $\sigma$, and a $95\%$ interval $\hat A\pm h$ would have half-width $h=1.96\,\sigma$, because $95\%$ of a normal distribution lies within $1.96$ standard deviations of its centre.
    2. *Add only the shift.* Keep the width, and so $\sigma=h/1.96$, but move the centre of the bell curve to the mean shift $\delta=\overline{\hat A-A}$. Measured in half-widths, the shift is $s=\delta/h$.
    3. *When the interval covers.* $A$ is inside $\hat A\pm h$ exactly when the error lies between $-h$ and $h$. Subtracting the centre $\delta$ and dividing by $\sigma$ turns the error into a standard normal variable, and the two limits into $(-h-\delta)/\sigma=-1.96(1+s)$ and $(h-\delta)/\sigma=1.96(1-s)$.
    4. *The probability.* With $\Phi$ the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal (the probability of falling below a value), the probability that the error falls between the two limits is $\mathcal C_s=\Phi\big(1.96(1-s)\big)-\Phi\big(-1.96(1+s)\big)$. It gives $0.95$ for $s=0$, about $0.83$ for $s=\pm0.5$, about $0.50$ for $s=\pm1$ (the true value on an end of the interval) and $0.025$ for $s=\pm2$.

    How to read it: coverage close to $\mathcal C_s$ means the width is right and the shift explains the whole loss; coverage below $\mathcal C_s$ means the interval is also too narrow for the spread of the error; coverage above $\mathcal C_s$ means that the shift is not the same in every bag (the formula assumes it is), or that the interval is wider than it needs to be. The formula assumes a symmetric interval and a normal error, and uses the mean amplitude of the pair; it is a reading aid, not a model of BayCAP. On `main` it follows the observed coverage closely: correlation $0.98$ over the $75$ pairs; e.g. `spambase` RF, $s=-0.89$, $\mathcal C_s=0.58$, observed $0.53$; `mammographic` MLP, $s=1.60$, $0.12$ against $0.10$; `dry-bean` LR, $s=-1.32$, $0.27$ against $0.29$. So the question is mostly what moves the centre.
  - **Cause 1, the prior on $C$, explains the largest displacements.** On `dry-bean`, `hand_digits`, `letter` and `isolet` ($K\ge7$, $\kappa_0\approx1$–$2$, $P\approx0$) the shift equals the recall term and the recall term equals its prior part: the points lie on $y=x$ in the cause-1 scatter. The coverage there is $0.00$–$0.33$, because the intervals are narrow ($0.014$–$0.049$) and $\lvert s\rvert$ ranges from $1.2$ to $8.9$. The size follows §T.5: `isolet` LR, $n^s_j\approx105$ and recall on $V$ $0.95$, gives $R_0=-0.181$ against a shift of $-0.178$; `letter`, with about $2.5$ times more validation instances per class, has shifts of $-0.065$ to $-0.084$. On `chess` ($K=15$) the recall term also follows the prior part, and the better classifiers cover worse (RF, accuracy on $V$ $0.62$, coverage $0.25$; LR, $0.33$, $0.88$): higher recalls are farther from $1/K$, so the prior pulls them more, and their intervals are narrower.
  - **Cause 2, conditioning, displaces only together with a systematic error.** Bad conditioning alone becomes width: `connect-4` LR ($\kappa_0=88.6$, amplitude $0.48$) and `shuttle` LR ($\kappa_0\approx3\cdot10^4$, amplitude $0.43$) cover at $0.98$ and $0.92$. It displaces where the prevalence error has the same sign in every bag ($\lvert P\rvert\approx\lvert\bar P\rvert$): `german` ($1/\sigma_{\min}=2.7$–$4.9$, $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1=0.20$–$0.44$, $P$ is $0.110$ of the $0.158$ shift of SVM, coverage $0.18$) and `wine-quality` ($\kappa_0=\infty$ for LR and SVM, LR $\bar P=0.202$, $\lvert P\rvert=0.203$, coverage $0.35$). Where $P$ changes sign across bags (`wine-quality` kNN, $\bar P=-0.034$, $\lvert P\rvert=0.059$) coverage holds ($1.00$). Why the prevalence error is systematic on a singular $\hat C$ is not explained: in the directions $\hat C$ cannot identify, the posterior falls back on the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior on $\pi^Q$, which matches UPP and should not bias the error on average over the bags.
  - **Cause 3, difficulty, does not act on its own.** A low accuracy on $V$ goes with under-coverage only through the conditioning (`german`, `wine-quality`, `poker_hand`); in the conditioning scatter the low-coverage pairs sit at $1/\sigma_{\min}\approx1$ and high accuracy on $V$, and on `chess` the relation is reversed. `poker_hand` (minimum $n^s_j=3$, $\kappa_0=\infty$ for every classifier, $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1=0.82$–$1.18$) covers at $0.68$–$0.88$ only because its intervals are very wide (up to $0.75$): they cover without informing.
  - **A recall error not explained by the prior remains on binary datasets.** On `mammographic` all five classifiers have a positive shift ($+0.034$ to $+0.071$) that is all recall term, with $P\approx0$ and $R_0\approx-0.005$ of the opposite sign ($R-R_0=+0.04$ to $+0.08$); coverage is $0.10$–$0.65$ except kNN ($0.89$). The same profile, on single classifiers, appears on `tictactoe` kNN ($R-R_0=+0.04$, coverage $0.66$), `semeion` LR and MLP ($+0.04$, covered by wide intervals) and `spambase` RF ($-0.01$ on an interval of width $0.029$, coverage $0.53$). The decomposition leaves two explanations, which E2.2 does not separate: the step in which the target bag moves the posterior of $C$, and a difference between the recalls of $h$ on $V$ and on the test pool from which the bags are drawn (cause 4 [new hypothesis]: the bags are drawn with replacement from a pool of $249$ instances on `mammographic`, so a sampling difference between $V$ and the pool is shared by all bags and all classifiers, and BayCAP's population interval does not account for the pool's own sampling error).
  - **Width falls short on a few pairs.** On $10$ pairs the observed coverage is more than $0.05$ below $\mathcal C_s$ (`spambase` SVM and RF, `connect-4` MLP, `nursery` SVM and MLP, `poker_hand` SVM, MLP and RF, `chess` LR and SVM; $9$ on the old results, with `chess` MLP in place of `spambase` RF), so the interval is also too narrow for the spread across bags. Candidate explanations, not tested: the bag-level fluctuation of the realised table (§T.2, the subject of E2.1 and E2.3), skewed posteriors near an accuracy of $1$ (`nursery` SVM and MLP, accuracy on $V$ $0.99$ and $0.999$), and NUTS sampling quality ($1000$ draws after $500$ warm-up iterations; multimodality when $\hat C$ is singular). Where $P$ changes sign across bags (`german`, `semeion`, `wine-quality` LR) the observed coverage is above $\mathcal C_s$, as expected, since the shift is not the same in every bag.

  What this shows: the under-coverage of BayCAP on `main` has two main sources. With many classes and few validation instances per class, the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior on $C$ pulls the recalls towards $1/K$ and moves the narrow interval away from the truth; this accounts for every pair with coverage near $0$. With a weak, badly conditioned classifier, a systematic error in the estimated prevalences displaces the interval when it is not wide enough. On binary datasets a smaller recall error remains that neither explains. The evidence is correlational (the prior was not changed). The realised-table predictive cannot repair any of the three, which is consistent with E2.1.

  The analysis of the coverage error stops here (decision of 2026-10-21); what it leaves open is recorded as a guess, not tested:
  - **the recall error on binary datasets** most likely comes from cause 4: the recalls of $h$ on $V$ and on the small test pool differ by chance, and the difference is repeated in every bag and for every classifier of the dataset, while the population interval does not account for the pool's own sampling error; the alternative is the step in which the target bag moves the posterior of $C$;
  - **the systematic prevalence error on a singular $\hat C$** (`wine-quality` LR and SVM) is guessed to come from the prior on $C$ rather than the prior on $\pi^Q$: when $h$ never predicts some class, the counts of the bag do not say how to attribute the predictions between that class and the classes it is confused with, and the imaginary counts of the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior on $C$ decide the attribution, in the same direction in every bag;
  - **the width that falls short on a few pairs** is guessed to be mainly the bag-level fluctuation of the realised table, which the population interval leaves out and the realised-table predictive adds; skewed posteriors near an accuracy of $1$ and NUTS sampling quality may contribute.

## E2.3: the exact realised-table predictive in `main`

**Why.** E2.1 approximated the realised-table predictive for accuracy (one $\bar\rho$ per bag, a normal in place of the sum of binomials, §T.4) and found that it would repair the coverage of only $5$ of the $53$ under-covering pairs ($3$ on the old `BayesCAP` results); the exact predictive tells whether the approximation is right. E2.2 found that most of the under-coverage of the population interval is displacement, and that on $10$ pairs the interval is also too narrow, possibly because it leaves out the bag-level fluctuation that the predictive adds. The realised pushforward changes the width and can also move the centre of the interval (§T.3), so repeating the E2.2 analysis on it tells whether it changes any of the causes of the displacement, or only the width. Running it in `main`, as a method like the others, stores its results next to those of every other method, with the same bags and the same NUTS draws as the stored `BayesCAP`.

- **Design.**
  - **Inputs:** the `main` results of `BayesCAP` (population pushforward, stored) and of `BayesCAP-real` (realised pushforward, added to `main`: `methods/baycap.py:BayCAP` with `estimand="realised"`, the priors and NUTS settings of `BayesCAP`, $500$ warm-up iterations and $1000$ draws, and the default seed, so that its posterior draws are those of `BayesCAP` on the same platform and JAX version; the stored `BayesCAP` results were computed on another platform, probably the GPU, and are recomputed on CPU by the user's decision of 2026-10-22, so that the two pushforwards share their draws); the $100$ `main` bags of size $1000$ (UPP) of all $75$ pairs; the validation counts $f_{ij}$ as in E2.2; the per-pair CSV of E2.1 for the approximate coverage.
  - **Varied:** the pushforward, population (`BayesCAP`) or realised (`BayesCAP-real`); data, bags, priors, sampler and seeds are held fixed.
  - **Measures:** vanilla accuracy only. For both pushforwards: coverage, amplitude and Winkler score, and the predicted coverage $\mathcal C_s$; the error measures of E2.2 from each pushforward's mean table (shift, recall term, prior part, prevalence term and its mean absolute value, $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1$); for the realised pushforward, also the coverage that E2.1 predicted with the approximation. For the Winkler score, a paired test of the difference between the two pushforwards on each pair: the two are scored on the same $100$ bags with the same posterior draws, so for each bag $b$ the difference $d_b=\mathcal W_b^{\text{real}}-\mathcal W_b^{\text{pop}}$ is computed, and the test asks whether their mean $\bar d$, which is the gap between the two Winkler columns, is further from $0$ than the variation over the bags allows. It is a paired bootstrap: the $100$ differences are resampled with replacement $10{,}000$ times (seeded), and the mean of each resample is recomputed; the $2.5\%$ and $97.5\%$ quantiles of these means give a $95\%$ interval for $\bar d$, and the two-sided p-value is the share of resampled means, shifted to be centred on $0$, that are at least as far from $0$ as $\bar d$. Two-sided means it detects both a decrease and an increase. The test is on the mean because the Winkler score is a mean over bags, in which a few large misses rightly weigh more than many small width costs; a rank test (Wilcoxon signed-rank) was tried first and set aside, because it tests the typical per-bag difference, which on $26$ pairs has the opposite sign of the mean (many covered bags pay the extra width, a few missed bags gain a lot). The median of the $d_b$ is stored as a description of the typical bag. The p-values are adjusted with Holm's method over the $75$ pairs, so that testing many pairs does not produce significant results by chance. All bags of a pair share the same $V$ and test pool, so a significant difference holds for these bags, not necessarily for other validation sets. The diagnostics of E2.2 ($\kappa_0(\hat C)$, $1/\sigma_{\min}$, accuracy on $V$) do not depend on the pushforward and are carried over.
  - **Estimand:** population table for `BayesCAP`, realised table for `BayesCAP-real`; both scored against the realised table of each bag. The error measures use the bag's true class proportions and recalls, so they are an analysis, not a method.
  - **Cost:** one rerun of `main` on `bcuda`, which computes only `BayesCAP-real` because the results of every other method exist: one NUTS run per bag, $75\times100=7500$ runs. `main` stores the samples of all three metrics (vanilla accuracy, macro-F1, macro-K) for `BayesCAP-real`, as for every method.
- **Representation.**
  - **Comparison table:** one row per pair, grouped by dataset and ordered by $K$: coverage, amplitude and Winkler score of both pushforwards, the $95\%$ bootstrap interval of the mean Winkler difference and its Holm-adjusted p-value, and the approximate coverage of E2.1 next to the exact one.
  - **E2.2 table of the realised pushforward:** the table of E2.2, same layout (diagnostics, interval, error), computed on `BayesCAP-real`.

Results in `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/tables/pushforward/pushforward.csv`, `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/tables/displacement/BayesCAP-real/displacement.csv`, `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/plots/main/pushforward/` and `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/plots/main/displacement/BayesCAP-real/`. Notation as in E2.2; "population" is `BayesCAP`, "realised" is `BayesCAP-real`.
  - **The realised interval is wider, by exactly the bag-level spread.** On every pair the realised interval is wider than the population one, by a factor from $1.00$ to $2.12$ (median $1.11$). If the bag-level fluctuation is independent of the uncertainty on $(\pi^Q,C)$, the two spreads add as variances, $\sigma_{\text{real}}^2\approx\sigma_p^2+\sigma_g^2$, so the width should grow by the factor $\sqrt{1+(\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p)^2}$, with $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p$ from E2.1 (§T.4). The observed factor is within $\pm5\%$ of this value on every pair (median ratio $1.005$, correlation $0.998$): the realised pushforward adds the bag-level spread and nothing else to the width. The largest factors are on `letter` ($2.08$–$2.12$ except LR, $1.79$), `dry-bean` ($1.78$–$1.80$) and `hand_digits` ($1.75$–$1.78$), where many validation instances make $\hat\sigma_p$ small; the smallest on `connect-4` LR, `shuttle` LR and `poker_hand` LR ($\le1.01$), whose population intervals are already wide.
  - **The centre does not move.** The shift of the realised point estimate equals that of the population one within $0.009$ on every pair (correlation $0.9998$; the largest differences on `isolet`, where the shift is about $-0.17$). This confirms the hypothesis of §T.3: BayCAP chooses $\pi^Q$ so that the predicted shares $q$ match the observed ones, so replacing $q_i$ with $n^t_i/N^Q$ barely changes the mean. The diagnostics and the prior part $R_0$ are the same for the two pushforwards, since they depend only on $V$ and on the bags, and the split of the shift into $R$ and $P$ is the same within $0.007$ on every dataset except `poker_hand`. There the recall term of the realised table is about $0$ for every classifier (LR: $0.017$ for the population table, $0.000$ for the realised one) and the prevalence term carries the whole shift ($0.220\to0.238$). A possible reason, not checked: the column sums of a realised table are $\sum_in^t_i\rho_{i\cdot}$, the prevalences that the observed predictions imply, not the posterior $\pi$, and with $3$ validation instances in the smallest class the two can differ; the shift is the same, so this moves error between the two terms without changing it. The E2.2 scatters of the two pushforwards show the same picture: in the cause-1 scatter the points stay on or near $y=x$, except the `poker_hand` points, which drop from $R\approx0.01$–$0.02$ to $R\approx0$, and the `isolet` points, which move slightly towards $0$; the conditioning scatter keeps every point in place (its axes do not depend on the pushforward), and only the colours of the pairs whose coverage rises change.
  - **Coverage rises where the interval was only too narrow.** Mean coverage goes from $0.60$ to $0.68$; the pairs with coverage at least $0.95$ from $12$ to $28$; the pairs below $0.5$ from $26$ to $18$. No pair loses coverage (the largest drop is $0.01$, within the noise of $100$ bags), and $16$ pairs go from below $0.95$ to at least $0.95$: the binary pairs with a small shift (`spambase` LR, kNN, SVM and MLP, `semeion` LR, `tictactoe` LR and MLP, `mammographic` kNN, `german` MLP), all five `nursery` classifiers, `shuttle` kNN and `connect-4` SVM. Among the $53$ pairs below $0.90$, $12$ reach $0.90$ and $4$ fall within $[0.93,0.97]$ with a lower Winkler score (E2.1's criterion; $5$ for the approximation).
  - **Displaced intervals improve only in part, or not at all.** Coverage rises most where the interval was displaced by about one half-width and the bag-level spread is large ($\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p\approx1.5$): `dry-bean` from $0.21$–$0.33$ to $0.57$–$0.74$, `chess` RF from $0.25$ to $0.69$ and MLP from $0.51$ to $0.74$, `hand_digits` LR from $0.10$ to $0.42$. The shift is unchanged, but the half-width grows, so $\lvert s\rvert$ roughly halves (`dry-bean`: $1.22$–$1.59\to0.66$–$0.87$). Where the shift is several half-widths, coverage stays near $0$: `letter` and `isolet` ($0.00$–$0.01$; $\lvert s\rvert$ still $2.1$–$5.5$) and `hand_digits` kNN, SVM and MLP ($0.00$–$0.02$). Where the prevalence error has a fixed sign and $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p<0.3$, coverage does not move: `german` LR, SVM and RF ($0.54\to0.56$, $0.18\to0.19$, $0.68\to0.68$), `wine-quality` LR, SVM and MLP ($0.35\to0.36$, $0.68$, $0.61$), `poker_hand` ($0.68$–$0.88\to0.70$–$0.88$). The binary recall error of `mammographic` stays as well: LR, SVM and MLP go from $0.48$, $0.48$, $0.10$ to $0.54$, $0.60$, $0.17$, with the same shift.
  - **The Winkler score improves significantly exactly where the interval was displaced.** The paired bootstrap of the mean difference (Holm-adjusted, $0.05$) finds a significantly lower Winkler score on $31$ pairs, a significantly higher one on $9$, and no significant difference on $35$ ($22$ with a lower mean, $13$ with a higher one). The $31$ decreases are all of `dry-bean`, `hand_digits`, `letter` and `isolet`, `chess` RF and MLP, `mammographic` LR, SVM, MLP and RF, `spambase` RF, `tictactoe` kNN, `shuttle` RF and `german` LR and SVM; on `letter` and `isolet` the score falls by $0.58$–$0.78$ ($12$–$31\%$) although coverage stays at $0$, because the intervals miss by less. On $4$ of them (`spambase` RF, `chess` MLP, `mammographic` RF, `tictactoe` kNN) the median per-bag difference is positive: most bags pay the extra width, and a few missed bags gain much more. The $9$ increases are pairs that already covered (population coverage $0.92$–$1.00$: `semeion` SVM and RF, `shuttle` SVM and MLP, `wine-quality` kNN, `connect-4` kNN, `tictactoe` MLP and RF, `spambase` kNN), and are small, $+0.004$ to $+0.011$. The $35$ pairs without a significant difference are of three kinds: pairs whose coverage the realised interval repairs, where the penalties saved on the few newly covered bags about balance the width added on every bag (all of `nursery`, `chess` LR, kNN and SVM, `connect-4` MLP and SVM, `spambase` SVM, `mammographic` kNN, `tictactoe` LR), pairs whose interval barely widens (`poker_hand`, `wine-quality` LR, SVM, MLP and RF, `german` kNN and RF, `shuttle` LR and kNN), and pairs that already covered, where the cost of the extra width is not significant (`spambase` LR and MLP, `semeion` LR, kNN and MLP, `connect-4` LR and RF, `german` MLP, `tictactoe` SVM). With $10{,}000$ resamples the smallest raw p-value is $10^{-4}$, which Holm turns into $0.0075$ for $75$ pairs; this does not change any conclusion at $0.05$.
  - **The E2.1 approximation is accurate and slightly conservative.** The exact and approximate coverages have correlation $0.998$; the exact one is higher by $0.008$ on average, and the mean absolute difference is $0.013$. Only $3$ pairs differ by more than $0.05$, all with the exact coverage higher: `chess` RF ($0.69$ against $0.57$), `dry-bean` LR ($0.73$ against $0.66$), `mammographic` RF ($0.78$ against $0.72$). One $\bar\rho$ per bag and a normal in place of the sum of binomials are enough to predict the effect of the realised pushforward on accuracy.
  - **The width shortfall of E2.2 is mostly the bag-level spread.** Coverage still follows $\mathcal C_s$ (correlation $0.978$, against $0.979$ for the population interval). Of the $10$ pairs where the population interval is more than $0.05$ below $\mathcal C_s$, $7$ are not below it with the realised interval (`spambase` SVM and RF, `connect-4` MLP, `nursery` SVM and MLP, `chess` LR and SVM). This supports the guess of E2.2 that the missing width is mainly the bag-level fluctuation. The $3$ `poker_hand` pairs (SVM, MLP, RF) remain below, which fits the other two candidates of E2.2 (few validation instances per class, NUTS sampling quality), not tested here. The $5$ `hand_digits` pairs fall newly below $\mathcal C_s$, by small amounts (e.g. kNN, $0.02$ against $0.09$): there $s$ is $1.0$–$1.8$, where $\mathcal C_s$ depends on the tails of the assumed normal error, and a guess, not checked, is that the real errors have lighter tails than the normal.

  What this shows: the realised-table predictive is the right pushforward for the quantity that `main` scores, and it costs nothing in coverage. It adds to the interval exactly the bag-level spread, which repairs the pairs whose population interval was centred correctly but too narrow, and lowers the Winkler score significantly wherever the interval was displaced; where it raises the score, the increase is small and confined to pairs that already covered. It does not move the centre, so it cannot repair displacement: the causes found by E2.2 (the prior on $C$ for large $K$, a fixed-sign prevalence error with a badly conditioned $\hat C$, the residual recall error on binary datasets) are the same under both pushforwards, and `letter`, `isolet`, `hand_digits`, `german`, `wine-quality` and `mammographic` remain under-covered. The approximation of E2.1 predicts all of this within about $0.01$ of coverage.

## E2.4: BayCAP with an empirical-Bayes prior on the recalls

**Why.** E2.2 found that the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior on the columns of $C$ explains the largest displacements: on `dry-bean`, `hand_digits`, `letter` and `isolet` the shift is almost entirely the prior part $R_0$ of the recall term, because the prior pulls every recall towards $1/K$ (§T.5). E2.3 showed that the realised pushforward widens the interval but does not move its centre, so these pairs stay displaced: the fix has to act on the prior. An empirical-Bayes prior (§T.9) gives the recalls of a classifier a common $\operatorname{Beta}$ prior whose centre $\hat m$ (the typical recall) and strength $\hat\nu$ (how alike the recalls are) are estimated from the validation counts; each recall is then pulled towards the typical recall of the same classifier, up for some classes and down for others, so the pulls cancel on accuracy on average instead of adding up. The prior uses only the labels of $V$, like the rest of BayCAP, and changes only the concentration parameters of the Dirichlet prior on the columns of $C$, so BayesianCC is otherwise unchanged. The experiment first predicts the effect of the new prior in closed form, from the step in which $V$ updates the recalls (the check), then measures it with NUTS (the variant), and the check explains the variant.

- **Design.**
  - **Inputs:** the $15$ datasets, $5$ classifiers and $100$ UPP bags of size $1000$ of `main` ($75$ pairs); the validation counts $f_{ij}$, rebuilt as in E2.2; the results of four methods of `main`, all built from `methods/baycap.py:BayCAP` with the prior on $\pi^Q$, the NUTS settings ($500$ warm-up iterations, $1000$ draws) and the seed of `BayesCAP`: `BayesCAP` (population pushforward, $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior on $C$), `BayesCAP-real` (realised pushforward, $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$), `BayesCAP-EB` (population, empirical Bayes) and `BayesCAP-EB-real` (realised, empirical Bayes). `BayesCAP` is built from `BayCAP` instead of `cap-ml`'s `ACC_BayesCAP` (user's decision of 2026-10-26), so that it can store the NUTS diagnostics; `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real` are run again to store them, and their draws, and so all their stored measures, must be unchanged.
  - **Varied:** the prior on the columns of $C$, $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ or empirical Bayes, and the pushforward, population or realised, each method with its own run; data, bags, prior on $\pi^Q$, sampler settings and seed are held fixed, so with the same prior the two pushforwards share their NUTS draws (as found for E2.3). The empirical-Bayes prior gives column $j$ of $C$ the Dirichlet parameters $\alpha_{jj}=\hat\nu\hat m$ and $\alpha_{ij}=\hat\nu(1-\hat m)/(K-1)$ for $i\ne j$, where $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ maximise the beta-binomial likelihood of the diagonal counts of $V$, within $\nu\le10^4$ and $m\in[10^{-3},1-10^{-3}]$ (§T.9).
  - **Measures:** vanilla accuracy only, per pair.
    - *Check (no runs).* Three estimates of each recall from $V$ alone: the plain recall $f_{jj}/n^s_j$; the posterior mean under $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$, $\frac{f_{jj}+1}{n^s_j+K}$; the posterior mean under empirical Bayes, $\frac{f_{jj}+\hat\nu\hat m}{n^s_j+\hat\nu}$. For each estimate $\tilde r_j$ and each bag $b$, with true class proportions $\pi^{(b)}$ and realised recalls $r^{(b)}_j$, the recall error $\Delta^{(b)}=\sum_j\pi^{(b)}_j(\tilde r_j-r^{(b)}_j)$; per pair, its mean over the bags (the displacement it predicts) and the mean of $\lvert\Delta^{(b)}\rvert$ (the typical size of the error). The $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ version validates the check against the recall term $R$ of `BayesCAP`, since the check leaves out the step in which the target bag updates $C$. Also $\hat m$, $\hat\nu$, whether the fit reached a bound, and the standard deviation that empirical Bayes leaves out of the interval, $\sum_j\pi_j(1-w_j)\,\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$ with $w_j=n^s_j/(n^s_j+\hat\nu)$ and $\pi$ uniform, $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$ from the curvature of the log-likelihood at its maximum (§T.9).
    - *Variant.* For the four methods: coverage, amplitude, Winkler score and predicted coverage $\mathcal C_s$; the error measures of E2.2 (shift, recall term $R$, prior part $R_0$, prevalence term $P$ and its mean absolute value, $\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1$). Within each pushforward, the paired bootstrap test of the mean Winkler difference of E2.3 between the empirical-Bayes method and its $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ counterpart, Holm-adjusted over the $75$ pairs. The change of the recall term, $R$ of `BayesCAP-EB` minus $R$ of `BayesCAP`, against the change the check predicts, the difference between the mean errors of the empirical-Bayes and $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ estimates.
    - *NUTS diagnostics.* For the four methods, per bag: the number of divergent transitions, the largest split-$\hat R$ (one chain split in two halves) and the smallest effective sample size over the parameters of $\pi^Q$ and $C$; per pair, the share of bags with divergences, the largest split-$\hat R$ and the smallest effective sample size.
  - **Estimand:** population table for `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-EB`, realised table for `BayesCAP-real` and `BayesCAP-EB-real`; all four scored against the realised table of each bag (`true_cts`). $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ use only the labels of $V$, so the empirical-Bayes variants are methods. The check and the error measures use the bags' true class proportions and recalls, so they are an analysis, not a method.
  - **Cost:** four runs of `main` on `bcuda` with `JAX=cpu`, one per method, each $75\times100=7500$ NUTS runs: `BayesCAP-EB` and `BayesCAP-EB-real`, then `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real` after their stored results have been checked against a recomputation and deleted. The recomputation of `BayesCAP` on CPU took about $46$ minutes (work plan §I.2), so each run is expected to take about as long. The check costs nothing.
- **Representation.**
  - **Comparison table:** one row per pair, grouped by dataset and ordered by $K$: coverage, amplitude and Winkler score of the four methods; for each pushforward, the $95\%$ bootstrap interval of the mean Winkler difference (empirical Bayes minus $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$) and its Holm-adjusted p-value.
  - **Prior table:** one row per pair, same order: $K$, minimum and median $n^s_j$, $\hat m$, $\hat\nu$ (marked when at a bound), the left-out standard deviation; the mean and mean absolute recall error of the three estimates of the check; $R$, $R_0$ and $P$ of `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-EB`; the NUTS diagnostics of the four methods.
  - **Scatter, check against variant:** the change of the recall term predicted by the check on the horizontal axis, the observed one on the vertical axis, one point per pair, coloured by $K$, with the line $y=x$; a second panel with the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ check against $R$ of `BayesCAP`, which validates the check.
  - **Scatter, coverage:** coverage with the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior on the horizontal axis, with the empirical-Bayes prior on the vertical axis, one point per pair, one panel per pushforward, with lines at $0.95$ on both axes.
  - **The E2.2 table and scatters** of `BayesCAP-EB` and `BayesCAP-EB-real`, next to those of `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real`.
- **Analysis.** Run on `bcuda` by the user on 2026-10-26, JAX on CPU, `cap-ml` `bb20aa5`: the four methods stored for all $75$ pairs, `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real` rerun with the NUTS diagnostics; the E2.4 tables and plots and the E2.2 outputs of the two new methods generated on 2026-10-27 and copied locally with `make pull` (work-plan card WP-6, §II.14 of the work plan). Results in `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/tables/eb_prior/eb_prior.csv`, `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/plots/main/eb_prior/`, `<OUT_DIR>/uncawe/tables/displacement/BayesCAP-EB/` and `BayesCAP-EB-real/`. "Uniform" is the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ prior, "EB" the empirical-Bayes prior; "population" and "realised" are the two pushforwards; notation as in E2.2. The rerun of `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real` reproduces the scores used by E2.3 (`tables/pushforward/pushforward.csv`): the coverage of every pair is identical and the Winkler scores agree within $2\cdot10^{-15}$, so the E2.1–E2.3 analyses stand as written.
  - **The check predicts the variant.** Averaged over the pairs, the absolute value of the mean recall error $\Delta$ is $0.0122$ for the plain recalls of $V$, $0.0328$ for the uniform posterior means and $0.0126$ for the EB posterior means (mean of $\lvert\Delta^{(b)}\rvert$: $0.0164$, $0.0354$, $0.0169$): the EB prior removes the bias that the uniform prior adds to the recalls, and leaves the recall error that $V$ itself has. The uniform check against the recall term $R$ of `BayesCAP` has correlation $0.9987$ and slope $0.96$ (mean absolute gap $0.0023$), and the predicted change of $R$ from the uniform to the EB prior against the observed one has correlation $0.9995$ and slope $0.97$ (mean absolute gap $0.0012$). The step in which the target bag updates $C$ therefore barely moves the recalls: the effect of the prior on the centre of the interval is decided by the update on $V$, in closed form. The prior part $R_0$ falls from a mean absolute value of $0.024$ (uniform) to $0.0015$ (EB), while the part of $R$ that the prior does not explain, $R-R_0$, is unchanged ($0.012$ on average for both priors). The prevalence term also gets smaller, in mean absolute value from $0.020$ to $0.014$, and $\overline{\lVert\hat\pi-\pi\rVert_1}$ from $0.197$ to $0.168$: better recalls give a better inversion.
  - **The multiclass displacement disappears.** With the EB prior the shift is between $-0.010$ and $-0.002$ on `isolet` (uniform: $-0.18$ to $-0.16$), between $-0.006$ and $-0.002$ on `letter` (uniform: $-0.084$ to $-0.065$), between $0.000$ and $0.005$ on `hand_digits` (uniform: about $-0.02$), and between $-0.007$ and $-0.002$ on `dry-bean` (uniform: $-0.017$ to $-0.012$). With the realised pushforward, coverage goes from $0.00$ to $0.84$–$0.96$ on `isolet`, from $0.00$–$0.01$ to $0.92$–$0.96$ on `letter`, from $0.00$–$0.42$ to $0.73$–$1.00$ on `hand_digits` and from $0.57$–$0.74$ to $0.92$–$0.97$ on `dry-bean`; `chess` RF and MLP go from $0.69$ and $0.74$ to $0.97$ and $0.95$. Over the $50$ pairs with $K>2$, the realised coverage rises by more than $0.05$ on $31$ and drops by more than $0.05$ on $4$, and the Winkler score is significantly lower on $31$ and significantly higher on $1$ (`nursery` MLP); by $K$, the mean realised coverage goes from $0.68$ to $0.94$ at $K=7$, from $0.12$ to $0.89$ at $K=10$, from $0.85$ to $0.94$ at $K=15$ and from $0.00$ to $0.92$ at $K=26$. The largest decreases of the Winkler score are on `isolet` ($-5.6$ to $-4.7$), `letter` ($-2.3$ to $-1.3$) and `poker_hand` (down to $-1.3$). This confirms cause 1 of E2.2, now by an intervention and not only by a correlation: the uniform prior pulls every recall towards $1/K$, and a prior that pulls them towards the typical recall of the classifier removes the displacement.
  - **The population interval is now often too narrow, and the realised one is needed.** With the EB prior the population interval covers less than its shift predicts on many pairs: coverage is more than $0.05$ below $\mathcal C_s$ on $26$ pairs for `BayesCAP-EB`, against $10$ for `BayesCAP`, and only $3$ for `BayesCAP-EB-real` (correlation of coverage with $\mathcal C_s$: $0.85$ for `BayesCAP-EB`, $0.97$ for `BayesCAP-EB-real`). The EB prior removes part of the uncertainty on $C$ (the amplitude of the population interval falls from $0.118$ to $0.109$ on average), so the bag-level spread, which only the realised pushforward represents (E2.3), is a larger share of the error; the realised interval widens the population one by a median factor $1.16$ with the EB prior, against $1.11$ with the uniform one. Mean coverage over the $75$ pairs is $0.60$ (uniform, population), $0.68$ (uniform, realised), $0.70$ (EB, population) and $0.84$ (EB, realised); the pairs with coverage at least $0.90$ are $22$, $34$, $27$ and $52$; the pairs with coverage below $0.10$ are $14$, $13$, $6$ and $4$; the mean Winkler score is $0.98$, $0.84$, $0.38$ and $0.32$. With the realised pushforward the Winkler score is significantly lower with the EB prior on $40$ pairs and significantly higher on $10$ (Holm, $0.05$); with the population pushforward, $37$ and $11$. Of the $41$ pairs whose uniform realised interval covers less than $0.90$, $24$ reach $0.90$ with the EB prior.
  - **On binary datasets the EB prior does harm.** Of the $25$ binary pairs, none gains more than $0.05$ of realised coverage and $11$ lose more than that; the Winkler score is significantly lower on $9$, all by at most $0.012$ (`spambase`, `semeion` kNN and SVM, `tictactoe` LR, SVM and MLP), and significantly higher on $9$, by $0.15$ to $1.40$ (`german` LR, SVM and RF, `tictactoe` kNN, all five `mammographic` pairs). Two mechanisms are visible.
    - *$\hat\nu$ at the upper bound* (`mammographic` LR, kNN, MLP, RF, $\hat\nu=10^4$; SVM, $\hat\nu=532$). With $K=2$ the fit estimates the spread of the recalls across classes from two counts; if the two plain recalls differ by no more than binomial noise explains, the likelihood keeps increasing with $\nu$ (§T.9), and the prior fixes both recalls at $\hat m$. The prior then outweighs the $n^s_j\ge141$ validation counts of each class, and the realised amplitude falls to $0.45$–$0.59$ times the uniform one; realised coverage drops from $0.17$–$0.99$ to $0.00$–$0.15$. The shift barely changes ($0.033$–$0.071$ uniform, $0.041$–$0.078$ EB), and is neither the prior part nor the prevalence term (both about $0$): it is the residual recall error that E2.2 found on binary datasets (cause 4), which the uniform interval was wide enough to absorb in part and the EB one is not. The left-out standard deviation of the check, $\sum_j\pi_j(1-w_j)\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$, flags these pairs: it is $0.020$–$0.023$ on the four pairs with $\hat\nu=10^4$, the largest of all $75$ pairs (median $0.0015$). `hand_digits` MLP and SVM also reach the bound, but there all the recalls are about $0.99$, the left-out standard deviation is $0.0013$–$0.0014$, and coverage improves.
    - *A moderate $\hat\nu$ that adds a pull* (`german` LR, MLP, RF; `semeion` LR, MLP; `tictactoe` kNN; $\hat\nu$ between $4$ and $12$). The uniform prior on a binary column is $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$, two imaginary counts (E2.2: $R_0$ between $-0.001$ and $-0.006$); the EB prior pulls with $\hat\nu$ counts towards $\hat m$, so $R_0$ is slightly larger ($0.002$–$0.005$) and the shift grows by about $0.01$, while the amplitude falls by about $5$–$10\%$. On `german`, whose shift is mostly a fixed-sign prevalence term (cause 2 of E2.2), the realised coverage falls from $0.56$ to $0.35$ for LR and from $0.68$ to $0.50$ for RF.
  - **Strong pooling hurts a class whose recall differs from the others.** On `nursery` MLP ($\hat m=0.997$, $\hat\nu=166$) the realised amplitude falls to $0.34$ times the uniform one and coverage from $0.99$ to $0.34$, although the left-out standard deviation is small ($0.0007$): the uncertainty on $\hat m$ is not the cause. A guess, not checked: one class has a recall well below $0.997$, and the common Beta prior, which treats the recalls of all classes as alike, pulls it up with $166$ imaginary counts. The other $K>2$ pairs that lose coverage are small losses on pairs that covered ($\le0.08$: `shuttle` MLP, `nursery` LR, `chess` LR).
  - **Concentrations below $1$ break the sampler.** The share of bags with at least one divergent transition is $0.013$ on average for the uniform prior (at most $0.36$ for a pair) and $0.284$ for the EB prior. The smallest concentration of the EB prior, $\min\{\hat\nu\hat m,\hat\nu(1-\hat m)/(K-1)\}$, separates the two cases: the $16$ pairs where it is at least $1$ (all of `spambase`, `german` and `mammographic`, `hand_digits` MLP and SVM) have no divergence, and all $18$ pairs with divergences in more than half the bags have it below $0.17$ (all of `shuttle` and `isolet`, `poker_hand` LR, kNN, SVM, RF, `nursery` RF and LR, `chess` MLP, `letter` LR). For an accurate classifier with many classes the off-diagonal concentrations $\hat\nu(1-\hat m)/(K-1)$ are small (`isolet` SVM: $26\cdot0.046/25\approx0.05$), and a Dirichlet density with a parameter below $1$ grows without bound near the faces of the simplex (§T.9, "Small concentrations"); the sampler, which works in unconstrained coordinates, meets a region of high curvature there and its steps diverge. On `isolet` the chains do not mix: largest split-$\hat R$ $2.6$–$2.9$ and smallest effective sample size $3$ for SVM, MLP and RF, and NaN for LR and kNN, probably because some entries of $C$ stay at the same value in every draw (not checked). The uniform prior has its own weaker diagnostics, mostly on `poker_hand`, `shuttle` and `wine-quality` (largest split-$\hat R$ above $1.05$ on $7$ pairs, smallest effective sample size below $100$ on $18$), but no pair with divergences in most bags. The EB results of the $19$ pairs with divergences in more than half the bags or a split-$\hat R$ above $1.05$ or NaN (the $18$ above and `wine-quality` kNN) come from chains that may not represent the posterior, so their coverage is not evidence for the prior: this includes all of `isolet` and most of `poker_hand`. On the other $56$ pairs, the mean realised coverage is $0.71$ with the uniform prior and $0.80$ with the EB prior, and on their $31$ pairs with $K>2$ it is $0.60$ and $0.90$.

  What this shows: the uniform prior on $C$ is the cause of the large-$K$ displacement, and an empirical-Bayes prior fitted on $V$ removes it; the check computed from $V$ alone predicts the change of the recall term almost exactly, so the effect of a prior on $C$ on the centre of the interval can be predicted without running NUTS. With the EB prior the realised pushforward becomes necessary, because the uncertainty left on $C$ is smaller and the bag-level spread is a larger part of the error; together, `BayesCAP-EB-real` raises the pairs with coverage at least $0.90$ from $34$ to $52$ and lowers the mean Winkler score from $0.84$ to $0.32$. Two problems of the prior remain. With $K=2$ the fit has two counts to estimate a spread, and $\hat\nu$ can run to its upper bound and make the interval too narrow (`mammographic`), or add a pull the uniform prior did not have (`german`); the uniform prior was harmless there. With many classes and accurate classifiers the off-diagonal concentrations fall below $1$, NUTS diverges, and on `isolet` and `poker_hand` the gain cannot yet be trusted.

  Why the two problems arise (explanation added on 2026-10-28; §T.10–§T.12, toy in Appendix A.2):

  - *With two classes the strength $\hat\nu$ is decided by one number.* The fit separates the true spread of the recalls from the binomial noise of $V$ by comparing how far apart the plain recalls are with how far apart noise alone would put them; with $K=2$ this is the single difference $\hat r_1-\hat r_2$, and when it is no larger than about one standard deviation of noise the likelihood keeps increasing with $\nu$ and $\hat\nu$ runs to the bound. Under a normal approximation this happens with probability about $0.68$ when the two recalls are equal, and still about $0.5$ when they differ by about one standard deviation of noise (§T.10); the toy finds $84\%$ and $56\%$ of its fits at the bound in these two cases.
  - *At the bound the plug-in prior removes the uncertainty of the centre.* $\hat\nu=10^4$ gives each recall a prior standard deviation of about $0.004$, while $\hat m$, estimated from the two classes of $V$, has a standard deviation of about $0.02$ on `mammographic` (the left-out standard deviation); the interval keeps the first and drops the second. In the toy this alone lowers the realised coverage from $0.95$ to $0.72$ even when the two recalls are truly equal, i.e. when pooling is correct; a prior with the strength $\nu^*$ that adds $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)^2$ to its variance gives $0.88$, with the Winkler score of the uniform prior (§T.10).
  - *With a moderate $\hat\nu$ the prior pulls the two recalls together.* With two classes the recalls are the true-positive and the true-negative rates, which differ for a structural reason (the decision threshold of $h$ and the class balance of $L$), not by chance as the exchangeability of the empirical-Bayes model assumes; pulling both towards their mean adds to accuracy a bias proportional to $(\pi_1-\pi_2)(\hat r_2-\hat r_1)$, which depends on the bag (§T.10). This is the pattern of `german`.
  - *On `mammographic` the prior exposes a displacement it does not create.* The shift there is the residual recall error of E2.2, which §T.13 attributes to the finite test pool (cause 4); the uniform interval was wide enough to absorb part of it, the narrow EB interval is not. In the toy, a pool of the size of `mammographic` lowers the realised coverage of the uniform prior from $0.95$ to $0.85$, and of the EB prior from $0.72$ to $0.52$ (Appendix A.2).
  - *With two classes there was little to gain.* The uniform prior adds only two imaginary instances per column, and in the toy with an infinite pool its realised coverage is $0.94$–$0.95$: the pull towards $1/K$ that the empirical-Bayes prior removes is negligible at $K=2$.
  - *Concentrations below $1$ make the prior singular at the faces of the simplex.* A Dirichlet entry with parameter $\alpha<1$ has about $\varepsilon^\alpha$ of its mass below $\varepsilon$ (half below $10^{-6}$ for $\alpha=0.05$), so in the logarithmic coordinates in which NUTS moves it has a tail of length about $1/\alpha$ next to entries that $V$ fixes within a few hundredths; one step size cannot serve both, and the steps diverge (§T.11). Raising each concentration to $1$ would undo the prior, because by aggregation the recall would then get $\operatorname{Beta}(\hat\nu\hat m,K-1)$ and the pull towards a low value would return; a prior that keeps the Beta on the recall and puts a separate prior on how the mistakes are shared among the wrong classes avoids both (§T.11).

## E2.5: the empirical-Bayes prior on all the binary datasets of `quapy`

**Why.** E2.4 found that the empirical-Bayes prior harms binary datasets, through two mechanisms: $\hat\nu$ at its upper bound, which makes the interval too narrow (`mammographic`), and a moderate $\hat\nu$ that pulls the two recalls together (`german`, `tictactoe` kNN). §T.10 and the toy of Appendix A.2 explain both as properties of the fit with $K=2$: $\hat\nu$ is decided by a single difference of two recalls, the plug-in prior ignores the uncertainty of $\hat m$, and the two recalls of a binary problem are not exchangeable. So the EB prior is compared with a baseline that is already displaced, for a reason that no prior on $C$ removes.

The $20$ EB pairs with divergences in more than $10\%$ of the bags all have $\min(\hat\nu\hat m,\hat\nu(1-\hat m))<1$, the singularity of §T.11, which at $K=2$ arises when a small $\hat\nu$ (a large gap) meets $\hat m$ near $1$ ($50$ pairs have a parameter below $1$). The divergences of the uniform prior on the small datasets are new (in E2.4 the binary pairs had almost none); a guess, not checked: a few dozen validation instances, often all correct, against a bag of $1000$ instances give a posterior with a narrow ridge. On `iris.1` the EB prior covers $1.00$ with a Winkler score of $0.010$, against $0.08$ and $0.226$ for the uniform prior, but its sampler diverges in every bag and its fit is degenerate: the pool is perfectly classified, the degenerate prior happens to say so, and the result is not evidence for the prior.

  What this shows: the binary failure of the EB prior found by E2.4 is general, and it is the failure of the fit that §T.10 describes. Wherever the two recalls of $V$ differ by less than about two standard deviations of noise, $\hat\nu$ often reaches its bound, the prior then fixes both recalls at $\hat m$, and the realised coverage falls by a third on average when $\lvert z\rvert<1$; the gap statistic $z$, computed from $V$ alone, tells these pairs apart. Where $\lvert z\rvert\ge2$ the EB prior is about neutral (large pools: coverage $-0.005$, Winkler score lower on $12$ pairs, higher on $6$). With a finite $\hat\nu$ the pull that §T.10 predicted is small, because $\hat\nu$ falls as the gap grows and the pull cancels over bags; the harm comes instead from a narrower interval around a centre that is already displaced. Most of that displacement is shared by both priors and is not due to the prior: with test pools of a few hundred instances or fewer, the recalls of the classifier on the pool differ from those on $V$, all bags inherit the difference, and the uniform prior covers $0.85$ with pools of at least $100$ instances and $0.67$ with smaller ones. The EB prior helps where it removes a large uniform pull, i.e. high recalls with few validation instances per class. For $K=2$ the uniform prior remains the better default, and the remaining binary under-coverage of both priors points to cause 4.

## E2.6: zero-cost screens for a hierarchical prior on the recalls

**Why.** E2.4 and E2.5 showed that the empirical-Bayes prior removes the large-$K$ displacement of the uniform prior but fails in two ways that come from plugging in the fitted $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$: with two classes $\hat\nu$ often reaches its bound and the interval collapses (§T.10), and when a Beta parameter falls below $1$ NUTS diverges (§T.11). A hierarchical prior, which gives $(m,\nu)$ a prior of their own and lets the posterior average over them, includes by construction the uncertainty that the plug-in prior drops; in the toy of Appendix A.4 it covers $0.94$–$0.97$ at every $K$, and gains most over the plug-in prior at $K=3$–$7$. The user decided on 2026-10-29 that BayCAP may change its model, and is to be presented as a Bayesian CAP method of its own, inspired by BayesianCC rather than identical to it; the hierarchical prior is therefore a candidate method. Before implementing it in NUTS, four possible problems can be screened at no cost, from the validation counts and the stored results: whether its answer is decided by the prior on $\nu$ rather than by the data (D1); whether the classes of a classifier are alike enough to be pooled (D2); whether its posterior has the geometry that made NUTS diverge with the empirical-Bayes prior (D3); and whether the cheap prediction of its effect, computed from $V$ alone, can be trusted (D4). D4 comes first, because it says how far the predictions of D1–D3 can be read as predictions of the method. The user decided on 2026-10-29 that the binary datasets of E2.5 stay confined to E2.5: E2.6 uses the pairs of `main` only.

- **Design.**
  - **Inputs:** the validation counts $f_{ij}$ of the $75$ pairs of `main` (rebuilt from the pretrain files, as in E2.2); the stored results of `BayesCAP`, `BayesCAP-real`, `BayesCAP-EB` and `BayesCAP-EB-real` in `main` on the same pairs: the accuracy draws of each bag (`estim_acc_ranges`), the estimated and true tables (`estim_cts`, `true_cts`) and the NUTS diagnostics.
  - **Varied:** the prior on $\nu$, among three proper priors on $\nu\in[0.1,10^4]$, the range of the toy of Appendix A.4: $p(\nu)\propto\nu^{-3/2}$ (the prior of Gelman et al., uniform in $1/\sqrt\nu$), log-uniform ($p(\nu)\propto1/\nu$) and flat in $\nu$. Held fixed: a uniform prior on $m\in(0,1)$; the grid of Appendix A.4 ($200$ values of $m$ from $0.0025$ to $0.9975$, $200$ values of $\ln\nu$ evenly spaced on $[\ln0.1,\ln10^4]$); the split of each column into the recall and a $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ share of the mistakes (§T.14), which does not enter the recalls or accuracy.
  - **Measures:** vanilla accuracy only, per pair.
    - *D4, the prediction of a prior's effect from $V$ alone, validated on the stored empirical-Bayes results.* For each bag, the stored accuracy draws of the method with the uniform prior are moved and rescaled to those the method would have with another prior (§T.14): the centre moves by the change of the posterior-mean recalls weighted by the estimated prevalence of the bag (from `estim_cts`, label-free), and the spread changes by replacing the variance that comes from $C$ under the uniform prior with that under the other prior. From the predicted draws: coverage, amplitude and Winkler score per pair, for both pushforwards. Validation: the prediction for the empirical-Bayes prior against the stored `BayesCAP-EB` and `BayesCAP-EB-real` (per pair, the difference of predicted and stored coverage, amplitude and Winkler score; across pairs, their correlation); then the same prediction for each hierarchical prior.
    - *Diagnosis of D4: does it miss because it keeps the estimated prevalences fixed?* D4 changes only the recalls, but the stored empirical-Bayes prior also changes the off-diagonal cells of $C$, and through them the estimated prevalences of the bag (§T.14). For each bag and pushforward, the change of the centre of accuracy from the stored uniform to the stored empirical-Bayes table is split exactly into a recall part $R$, the change of the recalls at the prevalences of the uniform method, and a prevalence part $P$, the change of the prevalences at the recalls of the empirical-Bayes method; both come from the stored posterior-mean tables (`estim_cts`), with no labels. Per pair: the means of $R$, $P$, $\lvert P\rvert$, of the shift of D4 and of $\lVert\hat\pi^{EB}-\hat\pi^{u}\rVert_1$; and the coverage, amplitude and Winkler score of two diagnostic versions of the D4 prediction for the empirical-Bayes prior: with the stored $P$ added to its shift (*D4+P*), and with its shift replaced by the stored change of the centre $R+P$ (*stored centre*), the spread rescaled as in D4 in both. They use the stored empirical-Bayes tables, so they explain the misses of D4 and are not predictions.
    - *D1, prior sensitivity.* For each prior on $\nu$: how much $V$ narrows the prior, the ratio of the $90\%$ widths of the posterior and of the prior of $\ln\nu$; the posterior mean of $m$ and the posterior mass at the upper end of the grid of $\nu$; the predicted change of the recall term from the uniform prior and the predicted standard deviation of accuracy from $C$, at the uniform prevalence and, as the largest value over classes, at the vertices $\pi=e_j$; across the three priors, the range of the D4 predictions of coverage and Winkler score.

  - **Estimand:** the predictions of D4 and their diagnostic versions keep the estimand of the method they start from: population table for `BayesCAP` and its predicted variants, realised table for `BayesCAP-real` and its predicted variants; all scored against the realised table of each bag. D1–D3 use only $V$; D4 uses the true tables for scoring, so it is an analysis, not a method.
  - **Cost:** none: no NUTS run; one grid posterior per pair and prior ($200\times200$ points), and $K$ more per pair for the leave-one-class-out p-values.

  - **Table, screens (D1–D3):** $K$, minimum $n^s_j$; for each prior on $\nu$, the width ratio of $\ln\nu$, the predicted coverage and Winkler score of the realised method; the smallest p-value and the largest $\lvert z_j\rvert$; $P(\min(\nu m,\nu(1-m))<1\mid V)$; the stored share of divergent bags of `BayesCAP-EB`.

  - **Scatter, diagnosis of D4:** realised pushforward, two panels with the line $y=x$: the mean stored recall part $R$ against the mean shift of D4; the absolute coverage error of D4+P against that of D4.
  - **Scatter, prediction:** predicted realised coverage with the hierarchical prior (log-uniform) against the stored coverage of `BayesCAP-real` and of `BayesCAP-EB-real`, two panels, coloured by $K$.
  - **Scatter, sensitivity:** the range of the predicted coverage over the three priors on $\nu$ against $K$, one point per pair.
  - **Scatter, exchangeability:** the smallest p-value (log scale) against the largest $\lvert z_j\rvert$, one point per pair.

- **How the results are read.** *D4:* if the predicted coverage of the empirical-Bayes method is within $0.05$ of the stored one on most pairs, and the predicted and stored Winkler scores agree on which pairs gain and which lose, the prediction is trusted for the hierarchical prior; otherwise D1–D3 are read only qualitatively, and the hierarchical prior must be judged by its own run. *Diagnosis of D4:* if D4+P is within $0.05$ of the stored coverage on the pairs where D4 misses, the misses come from the prevalences, i.e. from the off-diagonal cells of the empirical-Bayes prior, which the hierarchical model does not change (§T.14), and the D4 prediction for the hierarchical prior is more reliable than its validation suggests; if only the stored centre closes the gap, the misses come from the update of the recalls by the bag, which D4 ignores for both priors; if neither does, they come from the spread or the shape of the draws. *D1:* the sensitivity is expected to be large at $K=2$ and small for $K\ge7$ (§T.14); where it is large, the method's interval is decided by the choice of the prior on $\nu$ and of its upper bound, and the method needs a justified prior there or a different treatment of few classes. *D2:* pairs with a small p-value and a large $\lvert z_j\rvert$ are those where pooling would displace the bags dominated by one class; if they are many, the pooled prior needs a component for atypical classes. *D3:* if the indicator is high where the empirical-Bayes methods diverged, the hierarchical prior will need a reparametrisation of the recalls before NUTS; if it is low everywhere, the averaging over $\nu$ removes the problem.

# Theory

This part explains what the experiments need and nothing more. We follow the notation of the reference paper: $C_{ij}=P(\hat Y=\omega_i\mid Y=\omega_j)$ is the probability that an instance of true class $\omega_j$ is predicted as $\omega_i$, and $\pi^Q$ is the vector of class prevalences of the target bag $U$, with $N^Q=\lvert U\rvert$ instances and $K$ classes. The population table is $T=C\operatorname{diag}(\pi^Q)$: entry $(i,j)$ is the probability that a random target instance has true class $\omega_j$ and is predicted as $\omega_i$. Accuracy is the sum of its diagonal, $\sum_j C_{jj}\pi^Q_j$.

## T.1 BayCAP in brief

*Intuition:* BayCAP lists many plausible values of $(\pi^Q,C)$, computes the accuracy each one implies, and reports the range of the middle $95\%$.

BayCAP uses two kinds of counts. From the labelled validation set $V$ it uses $f_{ij}$, the number of validation instances of true class $\omega_j$ predicted as $\omega_i$, with $n^s_j=\sum_i f_{ij}$ instances of class $\omega_j$. From the unlabelled target bag it uses $n^t_i$, the number of target instances predicted as $\omega_i$.

**The multinomial distribution.** Roll a die with $K$ faces $n$ times, independently, with probability $p_i$ of face $i$, and count how often each face comes out, $x_1,\dots,x_K$. By independence, one particular sequence of rolls with these counts has probability $\prod_ip_i^{x_i}$, and there are $n!/\prod_ix_i!$ sequences with the same counts (the ways of choosing which rolls give which face), so

$$
\operatorname{Mult}(x\mid n,p)=\frac{n!}{\prod_ix_i!}\prod_ip_i^{x_i}.
$$

**The two likelihoods.** Within class $\omega_j$ of $V$, each instance is predicted as $\omega_i$ with probability $C_{ij}$, independently of the others: the predictions are rolls of a die with the probabilities of column $j$ of $C$. In $U$, a random instance is predicted as $\omega_i$ with probability $\sum_jC_{ij}\pi^Q_j$, by the law of total probability (the probability of an outcome is the sum, over the ways it can happen, of the probability of each way: here, true class $\omega_j$ with probability $\pi^Q_j$, then prediction $\omega_i$ with probability $C_{ij}$); in vector form, the predicted shares are $C\pi^Q$. The two likelihood terms say how probable the observed counts are for given $(\pi^Q,C)$:

$$
\mathcal L_V(C)=\prod_{j=1}^K \operatorname{Mult}\!\left(f_{\cdot j}\mid n^s_j,\,C_{:j}\right),
\qquad
\mathcal L_U(\pi^Q,C)=\operatorname{Mult}\!\left(n^t\mid N^Q,\,C\pi^Q\right).
$$

**The priors.** A Dirichlet distribution $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$ is a distribution over probability vectors $p$ (non-negative, summing to one), with density proportional to $\prod_ip_i^{\alpha_i-1}$. With all $\alpha_i=1$ the density is constant: every probability vector is equally likely. BayCAP puts a Dirichlet prior on $\pi^Q$ (uniform by default, `prior`) and the uniform $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ on each column of $C$ (fixed in the code).

**The posterior.** Bayes' rule combines prior and data: the posterior is proportional to prior times likelihood,

$$
p(\pi^Q,C\mid V,U)\propto\operatorname{Dir}(\pi^Q;\alpha)\prod_j\operatorname{Dir}(C_{:j};\mathbf 1)\;\mathcal L_V(C)\,\mathcal L_U(\pi^Q,C).
$$

It has no closed form, so a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler (NUTS) draws $R$ pairs $(\pi^{(r)},C^{(r)})$ whose histogram approximates it; by the law of large numbers, averages over many draws approximate averages over the posterior. Each draw gives a table $T^{(r)}=C^{(r)}\operatorname{diag}(\pi^{(r)})$ and an accuracy $A(T^{(r)})$. The point estimate is the mean of these accuracies, and the interval runs from their $2.5\%$ to their $97.5\%$ percentile.

*Implementation note:* in `cap-ml` and `quapy`, `p_c_cond_y` is indexed `[y, c]`, so it stores $C^\top$, and the tables returned by `ACC_BayesCAP` have true classes on rows.

## T.2 Population accuracy and realised accuracy

*Intuition:* even if we knew $\pi^Q$ and $C$ exactly, we would not know the accuracy on *this* bag, because the classifier is right on a random number of its instances.

- The **population accuracy** $\sum_j C_{jj}\pi^Q_j$ is the accuracy the classifier has on average over all bags with prevalence $\pi^Q$. BayCAP's posterior is about $(\pi^Q,C)$, so its interval is an interval for this quantity.
- The **realised accuracy** is the fraction of instances of this bag that the classifier gets right. It is computed from the bag's realised table (its true labels), and `main` scores against it (`true_cts`).

**One instance.** Let $B$ be $1$ if an instance of class $\omega_j$ is classified correctly and $0$ otherwise. It is $1$ with probability $p=C_{jj}$. Its mean is $\mathbb E[B]=1\cdot p+0\cdot(1-p)=p$; since $B^2=B$, also $\mathbb E[B^2]=p$; the variance is the mean of the square minus the square of the mean, $\operatorname{Var}(B)=p-p^2=p(1-p)$.

**One class.** The bag contains $n_j$ instances of class $\omega_j$, each classified correctly independently of the others. The number of correct predictions $c_j$ is the sum of their $n_j$ indicators, a binomial count. The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, and the variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of their variances, so $\mathbb E[c_j]=n_jC_{jj}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(c_j)=n_jC_{jj}(1-C_{jj})$.

**The bag.** The realised accuracy is $A_{\text{real}}=\frac1{N^Q}\sum_jc_j$. The counts of different classes are independent, so the same two rules apply again, together with $\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ for a constant $a$ (here $a=1/N^Q$). With $n_j=N^Q\pi^Q_j$,

$$
\mathbb E[A_{\text{real}}]=\frac1{N^Q}\sum_jn_jC_{jj}=\sum_jC_{jj}\pi^Q_j,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(A_{\text{real}})=\frac{1}{(N^Q)^2}\sum_j n_j\,C_{jj}(1-C_{jj}):
$$

the mean is the population accuracy, and the variance is the fluctuation around it.

*Example.* Binary, $n_0=n_1=500$, $C_{00}=0.9$, $C_{11}=0.7$. The population accuracy is $0.8$. The standard deviation of the realised accuracy is $\sqrt{500\cdot0.9\cdot0.1+500\cdot0.7\cdot0.3}/1000\approx0.012$, so on a typical bag the realised accuracy lies anywhere in $[0.776,0.824]$.

BayCAP's interval contains no term for this fluctuation. When $V$ is small, the uncertainty about $C$ is large and hides it. When $V$ is large, the interval becomes narrower than the fluctuation, and it misses the realised accuracy more often than $5\%$ of the time. This is the *estimand gap*.

## T.3 The realised-table predictive

*Intuition:* we cannot see the true labels of the bag, but we can see its predictions; for each prediction we guess a plausible true class, and the guessed labels give a plausible realised table.

**The probability of the true class given the prediction.** The definition of conditional probability is $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$. Take a target instance predicted as $\omega_i$. If $(\pi^Q,C)$ were known, the probability of "true class $\omega_j$ and prediction $\omega_i$" would be entry $(i,j)$ of the population table, $C_{ij}\pi^Q_j$ (T.1), and the probability of "prediction $\omega_i$" would be the sum of row $i$, $q_i=\sum_lC_{il}\pi^Q_l$ (the law of total probability of T.1). So

$$
\rho_{ij}=P(Y=\omega_j\mid\hat Y=\omega_i)=\frac{C_{ij}\,\pi^Q_j}{\sum_l C_{il}\,\pi^Q_l}.
$$

Row $i$ of $\rho$ is row $i$ of the table rescaled to sum to one. On the diagonal, $\rho_{ii}$ is the precision of class $\omega_i$.

**Sampling a realised table.** For each posterior draw $r$, the predictive computes $\rho^{(r)}$ from $(\pi^{(r)},C^{(r)})$ and assigns true classes to the $n^t_i$ instances predicted as $\omega_i$ by one multinomial draw, $\operatorname{Mult}(n^t_i,\rho^{(r)}_{i\cdot})$, i.e. by $n^t_i$ independent picks with probabilities $\rho^{(r)}_{i\cdot}$. The resulting count table, divided by $N^Q$, is a sample $\tilde T^{(r)}$ of the realised table, and $A(\tilde T^{(r)})$ is a sample of the realised accuracy. The posterior is unchanged; only the last step changes, at the cost of one multinomial draw per sample. The samples now contain both the uncertainty about $(\pi^Q,C)$ and the bag-level fluctuation of T.2.

**Where the predictive puts the centre.** Of the $n^t_i$ instances predicted as $\omega_i$, each is correct with probability $\rho_{ii}$, so for one draw the realised accuracy samples have mean $\sum_i\frac{n^t_i}{N^Q}\rho_{ii}$. The population accuracy of the same draw can be written in the same form: multiplying the definitions, $q_i\rho_{ii}=\sum_lC_{il}\pi^Q_l\cdot\frac{C_{ii}\pi^Q_i}{\sum_lC_{il}\pi^Q_l}=C_{ii}\pi^Q_i$, so $\sum_jC_{jj}\pi^Q_j=\sum_iq_i\rho_{ii}$, where $q_i$ is the share of the bag that the draw expects to be predicted as $\omega_i$. The two means differ by $\sum_i\big(\frac{n^t_i}{N^Q}-q_i\big)\rho_{ii}$, i.e. only where the observed shares $n^t_i/N^Q$ differ from $q_i$. BayCAP chooses $\pi^Q$ so that $q$ matches the observed shares as well as the model allows, so the centre is expected to move little and the predictive mostly changes the width [new hypothesis, confirmed by E2.3: the two centres differ by at most $0.009$]. In particular, the pull of the prior on $C$ towards $1/K$ acts on $C^{(r)}$, and so on $\rho^{(r)}$, and is expected to remain.

*Toy check* (`scripts/toys/ext2_estimand_gap.py`, toy result, binary, not BayCAP on real data): $C_{10}=0.1$, $C_{11}=0.7$, $\lvert U\rvert=1000$, $\pi^Q_1=0.5$ with fixed class counts, $n_s$ validation instances per class, uniform priors, $3000$ bags, Monte Carlo error about $\pm0.01$.

| $n_s$ per class | population interval vs population acc. | population interval vs realised acc. | realised-table predictive vs realised acc. | width (population / predictive) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $100$ | $0.94$ | $0.92$ | $0.94$ | $0.136$ / $0.147$ |
| $500$ | $0.95$ | $0.85$ | $0.94$ | $0.061$ / $0.081$ |
| $5000$ | $0.97$ | $0.63$ | $0.96$ | $0.027$ / $0.059$ |

With few validation instances the uncertainty about $C$ dominates and the population interval is nearly right also for the realised accuracy; with many, the estimand gap dominates and its coverage drops to $0.63$; the predictive stays near $0.95$ at every $n_s$, with wider intervals.

## T.4 The approximation of E2.1

*Intuition:* `main` did not store the posterior tables, so E2.1 cannot redo the predictive exactly; for accuracy, it adds to each stored accuracy sample a random error of the right size.

**The exact accuracy of one draw.** For accuracy only the diagonal matters. One cell of a multinomial is binomial (merge all the other faces of the die into one), so, of the $n^t_i$ instances predicted as $\omega_i$, the number assigned to the true class $\omega_i$ is $\operatorname{Bin}(n^t_i,\rho^{(r)}_{ii})$, with mean $n^t_i\rho^{(r)}_{ii}$ and variance $n^t_i\rho^{(r)}_{ii}(1-\rho^{(r)}_{ii})$ (T.2). The draws for different predicted classes are independent, so their variances add, and dividing by $N^Q$ divides the variance by $(N^Q)^2$:

$$
\tilde A^{(r)}=\frac{1}{N^Q}\sum_i \operatorname{Bin}\!\left(n^t_i,\rho^{(r)}_{ii}\right),
\qquad
\sigma_g^2=\frac{1}{(N^Q)^2}\sum_i n^t_i\,\rho^{(r)}_{ii}\left(1-\rho^{(r)}_{ii}\right),
$$

with mean close to the population accuracy of the draw (T.3) and variance $\sigma_g^2$.

**The two simplifications.** E2.1 uses one $\bar\rho$ per bag (the stored mean table `estim_cts`, each row rescaled to one) instead of one per draw. And it replaces the sum of binomials with a normal distribution: by the central limit theorem, a sum of many independent indicators is approximately normal, so the approximation is accurate when the counts are large. Each approximate predictive sample is then the stored accuracy sample plus independent noise $\mathcal N(0,\hat\sigma_g^2)$. Both $n^t$ and $\bar\rho$ use no target labels.

**How much wider.** Let $\hat\sigma_p$ be the standard deviation of the stored samples. Adding independent noise adds its variance, so the predictive samples have variance $\hat\sigma_p^2+\hat\sigma_g^2$. For normal samples the $95\%$ interval is the mean $\pm1.96$ standard deviations, so its width is proportional to the standard deviation, and the width is multiplied by

$$
\frac{\sqrt{\hat\sigma_p^2+\hat\sigma_g^2}}{\hat\sigma_p}=\sqrt{1+(\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p)^2}.
$$

The ratio $\hat\sigma_g/\hat\sigma_p$ therefore measures how much the predictive can widen the interval: a ratio of $0.3$ widens it by about $4\%$ ($\sqrt{1.09}\approx1.044$). The approximation does not apply to macro-F1 or macro-K, whose off-diagonal cells matter; `main` stores the macro-F1 and macro-K samples of `BayesCAP-real` from E2.3, which would allow checking it.

## T.5 Displacement and the prior on $C$ (cause 1)

*Intuition:* if an interval is centred on the wrong value, making it wider helps only when it becomes wide enough to reach the true value, and then it is too wide to be useful.

**Where the error goes.** Accuracy is the sum of the diagonal of the table, $A=\sum_j\pi_jr_j$, where $r_j=C_{jj}$ is the recall of class $\omega_j$: accuracy is the recalls averaged with weights $\pi_j$. BayCAP's point estimate has the same form, $\hat A=\sum_j\hat\pi_j\hat r_j$, with $\hat\pi_j$ and $\hat r_j$ read from its mean table. Adding and subtracting $\sum_j\pi_j\hat r_j$,

$$
\hat A-A=\Big(\sum_j\pi_j\hat r_j-\sum_j\pi_jr_j\Big)+\Big(\sum_j\hat\pi_j\hat r_j-\sum_j\pi_j\hat r_j\Big),
$$

so the difference splits exactly into two parts:

$$
\hat A-A=\underbrace{\sum_j\pi_j(\hat r_j-r_j)}_{\text{recall term}}+\underbrace{\sum_j(\hat\pi_j-\pi_j)\,\hat r_j}_{\text{prevalence term}},
$$

where $\pi$ and $r$ are the class proportions and realised recalls of the bag. The recall term is the error from estimating how good the classifier is; the prevalence term is the error from estimating how many instances of each class the bag contains. The split applies to the point estimate as it is, because the mean is linear: the accuracy of each draw is the sum of the diagonal of its table, and the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, so the mean of BayCAP's accuracy samples is exactly the accuracy of its mean table.

**The prior as imaginary counts.** A column $C_{:j}$ lists the probabilities that an instance of class $\omega_j$ is predicted as each of the $K$ classes. It works like a die with $K$ faces: each validation instance of class $\omega_j$ is a roll, and $f_{ij}$ counts how often face $\omega_i$ came out. The prior $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ says that every composition of the column is equally likely. Multiplying the prior density, proportional to $\prod_iC_{ij}^{1-1}$, by the likelihood of the column, proportional to $\prod_iC_{ij}^{f_{ij}}$ (T.1), gives a posterior proportional to $\prod_iC_{ij}^{f_{ij}+1-1}$, which is again a Dirichlet, $\operatorname{Dir}(f_{\cdot j}+\mathbf 1)$: the posterior parameters are the prior's plus the counts (*conjugacy*). The prior acts exactly like imaginary rolls added before the data, here one roll on each of the $K$ faces. The mean of a $\operatorname{Dir}(\beta)$ vector is $\beta_i/\sum_l\beta_l$ (T.9 derives it), a face's count divided by the total count, so the recall becomes

$$
\tilde r_j=\frac{f_{jj}+1}{n^s_j+K}
$$

instead of the plain $f_{jj}/n^s_j$; with $K=2$ this is Laplace's rule for a coin, $(h+1)/(n+2)$. The difference $\tilde r_j-f_{jj}/n^s_j$ is the prior part of the recall error, the quantity E2.2 measures. Averaged over possible validation sets, where $f_{jj}$ is on average $n^s_jr_j$ (T.2), it equals

$$
\mathbb E[\tilde r_j]-r_j=\frac{n^s_jr_j+1}{n^s_j+K}-r_j=\frac{n^s_jr_j+1-(n^s_j+K)r_j}{n^s_j+K}=\frac{1-Kr_j}{n^s_j+K},
$$

which is negative whenever the classifier beats guessing ($r_j>1/K$): every recall is pulled towards $1/K$. For a good classifier the pull is about $-K/n^s_j$: negligible for binary problems, large when $K$ is comparable to the number of validation instances per class.

**Why the data do not undo it.** Bayes' rule combines prior and data once, $\text{posterior}\propto\text{prior}\times\text{likelihood}$; the sampler converges to this combination, not to the true $C$. The prior fades only when the real counts $n^s_j$ greatly outnumber the $K$ imaginary ones. Moreover, "every column equally likely" is not neutral about the recall. One entry of a $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$ vector follows a $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_j,\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_i)$ distribution (T.9), so under $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ the recall is $\operatorname{Beta}(1,K-1)$, with density $(K-1)(1-r)^{K-2}$ and mean $1/(1+(K-1))=1/K$. Integrating the density from $x$ to $1$ gives $P(r_j>x)=(1-x)^{K-1}$, so $P(r_j>0.9)=0.1^{K-1}$: with $26$ classes the prior considers a recall above $0.9$ practically impossible. The target bag cannot correct this: its predicted counts depend on $C$ and $\pi^Q$ only through the predicted shares $C\pi^Q$, $K$ numbers summing to one, far too few to pin down the $K^2$ entries of $C$.

**Why the interval is displaced and not just wide.** By the variance of a Beta distribution (T.9), $ab/\big((a+b)^2(a+b+1)\big)$ with $a=f_{jj}+1$ and $b=n^s_j-f_{jj}+K-1$, so $a+b=n^s_j+K$, the posterior standard deviation of a recall is $\sqrt{\tilde r_j(1-\tilde r_j)/(n^s_j+K+1)}$, the usual standard error of a proportion with the imaginary counts included. The shift shrinks like $K/n^s_j$, the standard deviation only like $1/\sqrt{n^s_j}$, so with large $K$ the shift is several standard deviations. Averaging into accuracy makes it worse. If the random errors $\varepsilon_j$ of the recalls are independent with standard deviation $\sigma$, the error of the accuracy, $\sum_j\pi_j\varepsilon_j$, has variance $\sum_j\pi_j^2\sigma^2$, which is smaller than $\sigma^2$ because $\sum_j\pi_j^2<1$ when more than one class is present ($1/K$ for equal prevalences): the random errors partly cancel. The prior instead pushes every recall in the same direction, so with shifts $\delta_j$ of the same sign the shift of the accuracy, $\sum_j\pi_j\delta_j$, is a weighted average of them and does not shrink. The result is a narrow interval in the wrong place, and the predictive, which only adds spread around the same centre, cannot repair it.

Other symmetric priors with less total weight, such as $\operatorname{Dir}(1/K)$ (one imaginary count per column instead of $K$), are studied in [[ext5_baycap_priors_tempering|Extension 5]]. E2.4 tests instead a prior whose centre and weight are estimated from $V$ (§T.9): $\operatorname{Dir}(1/K)$ puts most of its mass near the edges of the simplex, which is hard to justify as a description of the classifier, while a prior estimated from the data says what $V$ says.

**The prior on $\pi^Q$ is not a cause.** `main` draws the target prevalences uniformly over all possible prevalence vectors (quapy's `UPP`), which is exactly BayCAP's default prior $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ on $\pi^Q$. When the prior matches how the bags are generated, the interval for $\pi^Q$ covers at the nominal rate on average over the bags, so this prior alone cannot explain the under-coverage.

## T.6 Other effects on the width

- **Fixed class counts (too wide).** $\mathcal L_U$ treats the class of each target instance as random, while APP bags have fixed class counts. With random classes, $n^t_i$ is a binomial count with $N^Q$ tries and probability $q_i$, so its variance is $N^Qq_i(1-q_i)$. With fixed counts $n_j=N^Q\pi_j$, $n^t_i$ is a sum of independent binomials, one per class, $\operatorname{Bin}(n_j,C_{ij})$, with variance $\sum_jn_jC_{ij}(1-C_{ij})$. Using $\sum_j\pi_jC_{ij}=q_i$, the difference is
  $$
  N^Q\Big[q_i(1-q_i)-\sum_j\pi_jC_{ij}(1-C_{ij})\Big]=N^Q\Big[\sum_j\pi_jC_{ij}^2-q_i^2\Big]\ge0,
  $$
  $N^Q$ times the variance of $C_{ij}$ across the classes, weighted by $\pi$. The model therefore expects more variation in $n^t$ than there is, trusts the counts too little, and makes the interval wider. In the toy, at $n_s=5000$, coverage of the population accuracy is $0.97$ on APP bags and $0.94$ on bags with random class counts, so the effect is small there. Candidate E2.c2 would measure it.
- **Uncertainty on $C$ (modelled).** A finite $V$ leaves real uncertainty about $C$; $\mathcal L_V$ represents it, and it dominates when $V$ has few instances per class.

## T.7 Conditioning of $C$ (cause 2)

*Intuition:* if the classifier behaves almost the same on two classes, its predictions say little about how many instances of each class the bag contains, and every small error in $C$ or in the counts becomes a large error in $\hat\pi$.

**A binary example** (illustrative numbers). With $\mathrm{TPR}=C_{11}$ and $\mathrm{FPR}=C_{10}$, the fraction of the bag predicted positive is $q=\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-\pi_1)+\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi_1=\mathrm{FPR}+(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\,\pi_1$ (law of total probability), so $\pi_1=(q-\mathrm{FPR})/(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})$. With $\mathrm{TPR}=0.9$ and $\mathrm{FPR}=0.1$ the denominator is $0.8$; with $\mathrm{TPR}=0.55$ and $\mathrm{FPR}=0.45$ it is $0.1$, and the chance error of $q$ on a bag of $1000$ instances, about $\pm0.016$ (the standard deviation of a proportion, $\sqrt{q(1-q)/1000}\approx\sqrt{0.25/1000}$), becomes about $\pm0.16$ on $\pi_1$. Any error in $q$, $\mathrm{TPR}$ or $\mathrm{FPR}$ is multiplied by $1/(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})$.

**Singular values.** For $K$ classes the role of $\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}$ is played by the singular values of $C$. They measure how much $C$ stretches or shrinks vectors: for every vector $v$, $\sigma_{\min}\lVert v\rVert\le\lVert Cv\rVert\le\sigma_{\max}\lVert v\rVert$, and both bounds are reached in some direction. Singular values are used rather than eigenvalues because $C$ is not symmetric: its eigenvalues can be complex and do not measure stretching.

**The condition number.** Suppose the predicted shares carry an error, $q+\delta q=C(\pi+\delta\pi)$. Since $q=C\pi$, subtracting gives $\delta q=C\,\delta\pi$, so $\lVert\delta q\rVert\ge\sigma_{\min}\lVert\delta\pi\rVert$ and $\lVert\delta\pi\rVert\le\lVert\delta q\rVert/\sigma_{\min}$. Also $\lVert q\rVert=\lVert C\pi\rVert\le\sigma_{\max}\lVert\pi\rVert$, i.e. $1/\lVert\pi\rVert\le\sigma_{\max}/\lVert q\rVert$. Multiplying the two inequalities,

$$
\frac{\lVert\delta\pi\rVert}{\lVert\pi\rVert}\le\kappa\,\frac{\lVert\delta q\rVert}{\lVert q\rVert},
\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}:
$$

the condition number $\kappa$ bounds how much relative errors grow when $\pi$ is recovered from $q$. An error $\delta C$ in the matrix adds, to first order, a term of the same form (a standard result of numerical linear algebra), so

$$
\frac{\lVert\delta\pi\rVert}{\lVert\pi\rVert}\lesssim\kappa\left(\frac{\lVert\delta q\rVert}{\lVert q\rVert}+\frac{\lVert\delta C\rVert}{\lVert C\rVert}\right),
$$

covering both chance errors in the counts and systematic errors in $C$, such as the prior's.

**Restricting to vectors that sum to zero.** Prevalences sum to one, so every error in $\pi$ or in $q$ sums to zero. The columns of $C$ sum to one, so for a vector $v$ that sums to zero the entries of $Cv$ sum to $\sum_i\sum_jC_{ij}v_j=\sum_jv_j\sum_iC_{ij}=\sum_jv_j=0$: $C$ maps such vectors to vectors that also sum to zero. E2.2 therefore uses $\kappa_0$, the condition number computed only over vectors that sum to zero; $\kappa_0\ge1$, with $\kappa_0=1$ for a perfect classifier.

**The absolute amplification** [new hypothesis, 2026-10-20]. With $K=2$ the vectors that sum to zero form a single direction, so $C$ acts on them as one number, $\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}$, and $\kappa_0=1$ for every binary classifier, however poor: a ratio of the largest to the smallest stretching is uninformative when there is only one stretching. The binary example above uses instead the factor $1/(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})$, which multiplies absolute errors. Its general form is $1/\sigma_{\min}$, with $\sigma_{\min}$ the smallest singular value of $C$ on the vectors that sum to zero: by the first inequality above, an error $\delta q$ in the predicted proportions becomes an error of up to $\lVert\delta q\rVert/\sigma_{\min}$ in $\hat\pi$. E2.2 reports both: $1/\sigma_{\min}$ compares pairs of any $K$, including binary ones, while $\kappa_0$ tells, for $K>2$, whether the amplification concerns only some directions.

**Limits of the measure.**

- It is not specific to accuracy. If two classes are completely confused but have the same recall, $C$ is singular ($\kappa_0=\infty$) and their prevalences cannot be told apart, yet the accuracy is still exactly identifiable, because exchanging prevalence between two classes with the same recall does not change it.
- $\hat C$ is estimated on $V$: a column of a rare class rests on few instances, so its $\kappa_0$ is noisy; $n^s_j$ is reported next to it.
- It tends to grow with $K$, because there are more directions in which two columns can be close; comparing the classifiers within a dataset avoids mixing it with $K$, which also drives cause 1.

**The prior and the conditioning** [new hypothesis]. The imaginary counts pull every column towards the flat column $(1/K,\dots,1/K)$, which makes the columns more alike and amplifies errors more. $\kappa_0$ cannot measure this. When all classes have the same $n^s_j=n$, the posterior-mean column is $\frac{f_{\cdot j}+\mathbf 1}{n+K}=\frac{n}{n+K}\hat C_{:j}+\frac{1}{n+K}\mathbf 1$, so the posterior-mean matrix is $\frac{n}{n+K}\hat C+\frac1{n+K}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$. On a vector $v$ that sums to zero, $\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top v=\mathbf 1(\mathbf 1^\top v)=\mathbf 0$, so the matrix acts as $\frac{n}{n+K}\hat C$: the prior multiplies every singular value on these vectors by the same factor $n/(n+K)$, and their ratio does not change. E2.2 does not measure this effect.

## T.8 Difficulty of the problem on i.i.d. data (cause 3)

*Intuition:* a classifier that is often wrong even without any shift carries little information about the classes.

The difficulty of the problem is measured by the accuracy of $h$ on $V$, which is drawn from the source distribution without any prior shift. No cross-validation is needed: BayCAP evaluates the classifier $h$ that is already trained, and its predictions on $V$ are stored; cross-validating on $V$ would train other classifiers on less data. Difficulty and conditioning are related but different: a weak classifier often has similar columns, but a $26$-class classifier with modest recalls can still separate the classes well. In a correct model, low accuracy alone widens the interval rather than displacing it, so if displacement follows the accuracy on $V$ but not $\kappa_0$, it points to a mechanism not described here.

## T.9 An empirical-Bayes prior on the recalls (E2.4)

*Intuition:* the $K$ classes are judged by the same classifier, so they tell us what a typical recall of that classifier looks like. If $25$ classes have a recall around $0.95$, the $26$th is very likely around $0.95$ as well, not around $1/26$. Empirical Bayes estimates the typical recall and how much the recalls differ from the validation data, and pulls each recall towards the typical one: strongly when the class has few validation instances, weakly when it has many.

The numerical examples of this section are illustrative, computed by hand, not results. We write $n_j=n^s_j$ and $f_j=f_{jj}$ for the validation instances of class $\omega_j$ and the correctly classified ones, and $r_j=C_{jj}$ for the recall.

**The model for the validation counts.** Each class is like a coin with unknown probability of heads $r_j$, tossed $n_j$ times. The randomness is in the instances, not in the classifier: $h$ is fixed, and a given instance is always classified the same way. What is random is which instances end up in $V$.

- *One instance is one toss.* An instance $X$ of class $\omega_j$ is a draw from the class-conditional distribution $P(X\mid Y=\omega_j)$ of the source. Its success indicator $B=\mathbb 1\{h(X)=\omega_j\}$ is a Bernoulli variable with $P(B=1)=P(h(X)=\omega_j\mid Y=\omega_j)=C_{jj}=r_j$: the recall is the share of the class that $h$ classifies correctly, and a random draw from the class lands in that share with probability $r_j$.
- *The tosses are independent and identical.* The instances of $V$ are drawn independently from the source, so the $n_j$ indicators $B_1,\dots,B_{n_j}$ of class $\omega_j$ are independent, each with probability $r_j$. This needs $h$ to be trained on data other than $V$, as in the paper's protocol; if $h$ had seen $V$, the indicators would depend on $V$ through $h$.
- *Counting the successes gives the binomial.* $f_j=\sum_kB_k$. By independence, a particular sequence with $f$ successes and $n_j-f$ failures has probability $r_j^f(1-r_j)^{n_j-f}$, and there are $\binom{n_j}{f}$ ways to choose which positions are the successes, so

$$
f_j\mid r_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n_j,r_j),\qquad P(f_j\mid r_j)=\binom{n_j}{f_j}r_j^{f_j}(1-r_j)^{n_j-f_j},
$$

with mean $n_jr_j$ and variance $n_jr_j(1-r_j)$, $n_j$ times those of one toss.

Two remarks. In an i.i.d. sample $n_j$ is itself random; the model conditions on it (how many of these $n_j$ instances are classified correctly), which is legitimate because $n_j$ carries no information on $r_j$. And $V$ is drawn once, so its $f_j$ is one number: the binomial describes how $f_j$ would vary over the validation sets we could have drawn, and this variation is the uncertainty on $r_j$. Recording the predicted class instead of "correct or not" turns each toss into the roll of a $K$-faced die with probabilities $C_{:j}$, and the column of counts into $\operatorname{Multinomial}(n_j,C_{:j})$, the likelihood $\mathcal L_V$ of BayCAP (§T.1); the binomial is that multinomial with all the wrong faces merged into one.

**The plain recall is the maximum-likelihood estimate.** The value of $r_j$ that makes the observed count most probable maximises the log-likelihood $\ell(r_j)=f_j\ln r_j+(n_j-f_j)\ln(1-r_j)$ (the binomial coefficient does not depend on $r_j$). Setting the derivative to zero,

$$
\frac{f_j}{r_j}-\frac{n_j-f_j}{1-r_j}=0\iff r_j=\frac{f_j}{n_j}=\hat r_j .
$$

Since $\mathbb E[f_j]=n_jr_j$ and $\operatorname{Var}(f_j)=n_jr_j(1-r_j)$, the plain recall is unbiased, $\mathbb E[\hat r_j]=r_j$, with variance $r_j(1-r_j)/n_j$: right on average, but noisy when $n_j$ is small, and with no statement of its own uncertainty ($f_j=n_j$ gives $\hat r_j=1$ "for sure").

**A Beta prior and the posterior mean.** A Bayesian describes the uncertainty on $r_j$ by a distribution on $[0,1]$. The Beta distribution has density $p(r)=r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}/B(a,b)$, with $B(a,b)=\int_0^1r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}dr=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$. Using $\Gamma(x+1)=x\,\Gamma(x)$, its mean and variance are

$$
\mathbb E[r]=\frac{B(a+1,b)}{B(a,b)}=\frac{a}{a+b},\qquad \operatorname{Var}[r]=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^2(a+b+1)} .
$$

We write $a=\nu m$ and $b=\nu(1-m)$: the **centre** $m=\mathbb E[r]$ is the recall the prior expects, and the **strength** $\nu=a+b$ sets how concentrated the prior is, $\operatorname{Var}[r]=m(1-m)/(\nu+1)$. By Bayes' theorem the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior,

$$
p(r_j\mid f_j)\propto r_j^{f_j}(1-r_j)^{n_j-f_j}\cdot r_j^{a-1}(1-r_j)^{b-1}=r_j^{f_j+a-1}(1-r_j)^{n_j-f_j+b-1},
$$

which is again a Beta, $\operatorname{Beta}(f_j+a,\,n_j-f_j+b)$ (conjugacy). The update adds the observed successes to $a$ and the observed failures to $b$: the prior acts as $\nu$ imaginary validation instances, a share $m$ of which are correct. As a single estimate we take the posterior mean, which minimises the expected squared error, since $\mathbb E[(r_j-c)^2\mid f_j]=\operatorname{Var}(r_j\mid f_j)+(\mathbb E[r_j\mid f_j]-c)^2$ is smallest at $c=\mathbb E[r_j\mid f_j]$:

$$
\tilde r_j=\frac{f_j+\nu m}{n_j+\nu}=w_j\,\hat r_j+(1-w_j)\,m,\qquad w_j=\frac{n_j}{n_j+\nu}.
$$

**What the strength $\nu$ means.** Read $\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$ as the result of having seen $\nu=a+b$ imaginary instances, $a$ correct and $b$ wrong. The centre $m=a/(a+b)$, their ratio, says *where* the recall is; the total $\nu$, their sum, says *how much evidence* the prior represents. A frequency computed on more instances is less variable, and the variance of the Beta shows exactly this:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(r)=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^2(a+b+1)}=\frac{\nu m\cdot\nu(1-m)}{\nu^2(\nu+1)}=\frac{m(1-m)}{\nu+1},
$$

which has the form of the variance $m(1-m)/n$ of a binomial frequency on $n$ instances, with $\nu+1$ in place of $n$: the prior is about as informative as $\nu$ instances. So $a$ and $b$ separately are not the concentration; the same centre can be weak or strong (illustrative): $\operatorname{Beta}(8,2)$, $\operatorname{Beta}(80,20)$ and $\operatorname{Beta}(8000,2000)$ all have $m=0.8$, with $\nu=10$, $100$, $10^4$ and standard deviations $0.12$, $0.04$, $0.004$. "Concentration" means how tightly the distribution is packed around its centre, and it grows with $\nu$.

The posterior mean is a weighted average of what $V$ says and what the prior says, with the shares of real and imaginary instances as weights. For a fixed true recall, averaging over the possible validation sets ($\mathbb E[f_j]=n_jr_j$), its bias points towards $m$:

$$
\mathbb E[\tilde r_j]-r_j=\frac{\nu}{n_j+\nu}\,(m-r_j).
$$

**The uniform prior is the case $m=1/K$, $\nu=K$.** BayCAP puts $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ on the whole column $C_{:j}$. A Dirichlet vector can be built as $p_i=G_i/\sum_lG_l$ from independent $G_i\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha_i,1)$; the sum of the other entries, $G_{-j}=\sum_{i\ne j}G_i$, is $\operatorname{Gamma}(\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_i,1)$, and $G_j/(G_j+G_{-j})$ is Beta-distributed. The same construction gives the *aggregation property*: a sum of independent $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha_i,1)$ variables is $\operatorname{Gamma}(\sum_i\alpha_i,1)$, so if the entries of a $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$ vector are summed in groups, the group sums form a Dirichlet vector whose parameters are the sums of the $\alpha_i$ in each group. Its mean follows as well: $\mathbb E[p_i]=\alpha_i/\sum_l\alpha_l$, the mean $a/(a+b)$ of the $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_i,\sum_{l\ne i}\alpha_l)$ below. So one entry of a $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$ vector is $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_j,\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_i)$, and under $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ the recall is $\operatorname{Beta}(1,K-1)$: $m=1/K$ and $\nu=K$, chosen without looking at the data. The formulas above then give $\tilde r_j=\frac{f_j+1}{n_j+K}$ and the bias $\frac{K(1/K-r_j)}{n_j+K}$ of §T.5. As a check against the stored results, `isolet` LR ($K=26$, $n_j\approx105$, recall on $V$ $0.95$) gives $26(0.038-0.95)/131\approx-0.181$, the $R_0$ that E2.2 reports.

**Choosing $m$ and $\nu$ from the data.** Empirical Bayes assumes that the $K$ recalls of one classifier are themselves draws from a common Beta, as if the classes were coins from the same factory. The model has two levels, for each class $j$ of one classifier:

$$
\underbrace{r_j\sim\operatorname{Beta}\big(\nu m,\nu(1-m)\big)}_{\text{level 1: the recall of class }j}\ \text{independently},\qquad\underbrace{f_j\mid r_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n_j,r_j)}_{\text{level 2: the count on }V},\qquad j=1,\dots,K.
$$

- *Level 2* is the data mechanism above: each of the $n_j$ validation instances of class $\omega_j$ is a toss that $h$ gets right with probability $r_j$.
- *Level 1* is the assumption that the recalls of the $K$ classes come from a common Beta distribution: $m$ is the typical recall, and $\nu$ says how alike the recalls are. Here the Beta describes how the recalls of the classes are scattered, $\tau^2=\operatorname{Var}(r_j)=m(1-m)/(\nu+1)$, so $\nu=m(1-m)/\tau^2-1$, a decreasing one-to-one map: recalls that are very alike (small $\tau^2$) give a large $\nu$, recalls that differ a lot a small one, identical recalls $\nu=\infty$. A large $\nu$ says that the classes behave alike, so the typical recall is worth many instances of evidence about any one class. The assumption is that the classes are exchangeable: before looking at $V$, there is no reason to expect a particular class to be harder than another.

**The recalls are not observed, so they are integrated out.** We want the likelihood of $(m,\nu)$, i.e. the probability of the observed counts as a function of $(m,\nu)$. But $(m,\nu)$ do not produce $f_j$ directly: they produce $r_j$, which produces $f_j$, and $r_j$ is not observed. By the law of total probability, we average the binomial probability over all the values $r_j$ could take, each weighted by its Beta density (with $a=\nu m$, $b=\nu(1-m)$):

$$
P(f_j\mid m,\nu)=\int_0^1\underbrace{\binom{n_j}{f_j}r^{f_j}(1-r)^{n_j-f_j}}_{P(f_j\mid r)}\;\underbrace{\frac{r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}}_{p(r\mid m,\nu)}\,dr .
$$

This is called the *marginal likelihood*: the recall is "marginalised", integrated out. In words: the probability that a class with $n_j$ instances gets $f_j$ of them right, if its recall was drawn at random from this Beta. Integrating $r_j$ out is also what makes $\nu$ estimable. One could instead maximise the *joint* likelihood over the recalls as well,

$$
L_J(m,\nu,r_1,\dots,r_K)=\prod_j\underbrace{\operatorname{Binomial}(f_j\mid n_j,r_j)}_{\text{a probability}}\;\underbrace{\operatorname{Beta}\big(r_j\mid\nu m,\nu(1-m)\big)}_{\text{a density}},
$$

but its maximum is degenerate, whatever the data. Set all the recalls equal, $r_1=\dots=r_K=\bar r$: the binomial factors become fixed positive numbers. Now let $\nu\to\infty$ with $m=\bar r$: the Beta *density* at $\bar r$ grows without bound (roughly like $\sqrt\nu$), because a density is not a probability, and a distribution squeezed onto one point has an infinite density there. So $L_J\to\infty$, and its "maximum" is always "all recalls equal, $\nu=\infty$", even for recalls of $0.5$ and $0.99$. The joint likelihood mixes a probability (of the counts, at most $1$) with a density (of the unobserved recalls, unbounded), and maximising over unobserved continuous quantities exploits the unbounded part. The marginal likelihood is a probability of the observed counts, at most $1$, and cannot blow up: integrating the recall out forces the "factory" to put its mass on recalls that could have produced each count, so a very concentrated factory is rewarded only if counts from a single recall are plausible. With $\hat r_1=0.70$, $\hat r_2=0.90$ and $n_j=141$ (illustrative), a factory concentrated at $0.80$ makes both counts very unlikely, since $0.10$ is about three standard deviations of binomial noise ($\approx0.034$) away from each; the marginal likelihood is small at large $\nu$ and its maximum is at a finite $\nu$. This is how the spread of the counts, net of their noise, determines $\hat\nu$. The recalls are not discarded: once $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ is fixed, BayCAP does not pick one value for each $r_j$ but samples the whole posterior of $C$ with NUTS, given $V$ and the target bag; the recalls are always quantities to average over, never parameters to optimise.

**Why the beta-binomial.** The procedure does not require a particular distribution; the beta-binomial is what comes out of two choices, and both are forced:

1. *The binomial is forced by the data.* Counting the correct instances out of $n_j$ independent tosses gives a binomial (above). We look only at "correct or not", merging all the wrong classes into one face, because the prior we want to learn is a prior on the recall.
2. *The Beta is forced by BayCAP.* BayCAP puts a Dirichlet prior on each column of $C$, and by the aggregation property (above) the diagonal entry of a Dirichlet column is Beta-distributed. If we want to change only the concentration parameters of BayesianCC's Dirichlet, the prior on the recall must be a Beta; learning, say, a logit-normal prior would not fit back into a Dirichlet.
3. *Together, the integral has a closed form.* The binomial and the Beta have the same functional form in $r$, powers of $r$ and of $1-r$, so their product is $r^{f_j+a-1}(1-r)^{n_j-f_j+b-1}$, and its integral over $[0,1]$ is, by the definition of the Beta function, $B(f_j+a,\,n_j-f_j+b)$. Hence

$$
P(f_j\mid m,\nu)=\binom{n_j}{f_j}\frac{B(f_j+a,\,n_j-f_j+b)}{B(a,b)},
$$

the beta-binomial distribution. This is conjugacy at work: each term of the likelihood is a few log-Gamma functions, cheap to evaluate and to differentiate. With another prior the procedure would be the same (integrate the recall out, sum over the classes, maximise), but the integral would have to be computed numerically, and the result would not fit into BayesianCC.

**The likelihood and its maximum.** The classes are independent, so the probabilities multiply and the logarithms add; dropping the binomial coefficients, which do not depend on $(m,\nu)$, the log-likelihood of $(m,\nu)$ is

$$
\ell(m,\nu)=\sum_{j=1}^K\ln P(f_j\mid m,\nu)=\sum_{j=1}^K\Big[\ln B\big(f_j+\nu m,\,n_j-f_j+\nu(1-m)\big)-\ln B\big(\nu m,\,\nu(1-m)\big)\Big],
$$

and $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ is its maximum, the pair that makes the observed counts most probable. This is maximum likelihood one level up, sometimes called *type-II* maximum likelihood. There is no closed form; `fit_beta_binomial` in `methods/baycap.py` finds the maximum numerically:

1. it changes variables to $\theta=(\operatorname{logit}m,\ln\nu)$, which can take any real value, while $m\in(0,1)$ and $\nu>0$ cannot;
2. it minimises $-\ell(\theta)$, which is the same as maximising $\ell$, with L-BFGS-B, a quasi-Newton method that walks downhill using the gradient, computed analytically with digamma functions (the derivatives of $\ln\Gamma$);
3. it keeps the search within the bounds $m\in[10^{-3},1-10^{-3}]$ and $\nu\le10^4$ (see the limit cases below);
4. it starts from the pooled recall $\sum_jf_j/\sum_jn_j$ and three starting values of $\ln\nu$, and keeps the best of the three maxima.

The cost is negligible.

**The law of total variance.** For two random variables $X$ and $Y$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big).
$$

It follows from three facts: $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mathbb E[X]^2$; the tower property $\mathbb E[g(X)]=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[g(X)\mid Y]\big]$ (average within each value of $Y$, then over $Y$); and the same variance identity conditionally, $\mathbb E[X^2\mid Y]=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)+\mathbb E[X\mid Y]^2$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)&=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[X^2\mid Y]\big]-\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big]^2\\
&=\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\Big(\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]^2\big]-\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big]^2\Big)\\
&=\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The variability of $X$ has two sources: how much $X$ varies when $Y$ is fixed, and how much the centre of $X$ moves when $Y$ changes. The same argument without the squares gives the law of total expectation, $\mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\big]$.

**Why rare classes count less.** Take $X=\hat r_j$, the plain recall on $V$, and $Y=r_j$, the true recall. Given $r_j$, $\hat r_j$ is a binomial frequency, so $\mathbb E[\hat r_j\mid r_j]=r_j$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\hat r_j\mid r_j)=r_j(1-r_j)/n_j$. By the laws of total expectation and total variance, and with $\mathbb E[r_j(1-r_j)]=m(1-m)-\operatorname{Var}(r_j)=m(1-m)\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}$, the plain recalls have mean and variance

$$
\mathbb E[\hat r_j]=m,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat r_j)=\underbrace{\mathbb E\Big[\tfrac{r_j(1-r_j)}{n_j}\Big]}_{\text{binomial noise}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(r_j)}_{\text{true spread}}=\frac{m(1-m)}{\nu+1}\cdot\frac{n_j+\nu}{n_j}.
$$

The observed recalls are scattered for two reasons: the true recalls differ (the true spread, which carries $\nu$), and each is measured with the counting noise of $n_j$ instances. A class with few instances has a noisier $\hat r_j$. Approximating each $\hat r_j$ as normal with these moments, the log-likelihood becomes a sum of squares $-\frac12\sum_j(\hat r_j-m)^2/\operatorname{Var}(\hat r_j)$ plus terms in $\nu$, and its maximum in $m$ is a weighted mean, $\hat m\approx\sum_jw_j\hat r_j/\sum_jw_j$, with the weights $w_j=n_j/(n_j+\nu)$ of the posterior mean. The exact maximum does the same automatically. A simpler method of moments (the plain average of the $\hat r_j$ for $m$, and the spread across classes minus the average binomial noise for the variance) gives every class the same weight; in an illustrative example with $n_j=200,200,200,5$ and $f_j=190,192,188,3$, it puts $m$ at $0.86$, dragged down by the class with $5$ instances, while the weighted mean with a large $\nu$ is $573/605\approx0.947$. Since the pairs of `main` have very unequal $n^s_j$ (down to $3$ on `poker_hand`), E2.4 uses maximum likelihood.

**The limit cases.** If the counts vary no more than binomial noise alone explains, the likelihood keeps increasing with $\nu$: as $\nu\to\infty$ the prior collapses on $m$, the beta-binomial becomes $\operatorname{Binomial}(n_j,m)$, the maximum is the pooled recall $\hat m=\sum_jf_j/\sum_jn_j$, and every class gets it. If the classes truly differ a lot, $\hat\nu$ is small, $w_j\approx1$, and each class keeps its plain recall. With $K=2$ two counts determine two parameters, and $\hat\nu$ often lands on one of the two limits; at $K=2$ the uniform prior was already harmless (E2.2: $R_0$ between $-0.001$ and $-0.006$). If every recall on $V$ is $1$, $\hat m\to1$. The fit is therefore bounded, $\nu\le10^4$ and $m\in[10^{-3},1-10^{-3}]$, and E2.4 reports the pairs where it reaches a bound.

**From the Beta to a prior on the whole column.** BayCAP needs a prior on each column of $C$, not only on its diagonal entry, because it estimates $\pi^Q$ from $q=C\pi^Q$. By the aggregation property above, the Dirichlet with

$$
\alpha_{jj}=\hat\nu\hat m,\qquad \alpha_{ij}=\frac{\hat\nu(1-\hat m)}{K-1}\quad(i\ne j)
$$

has diagonal entry $C_{jj}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\hat\nu\hat m,\hat\nu(1-\hat m))$, since the other parameters sum to $\hat\nu(1-\hat m)$. Only the concentration parameters change; the likelihoods of $V$ and of the target counts, the prior on $\pi^Q$ and the sampler are those of BayesianCC. By conjugacy, $V$ turns the prior into $\operatorname{Dir}(f_{\cdot j}+\alpha_{\cdot j})$, whose diagonal mean is $\tilde r_j=\frac{f_j+\hat\nu\hat m}{n_j+\hat\nu}$; the target bag then updates $C$ and $\pi^Q$ together, as before. Two consequences:

- *The spread of the mistakes is fixed by $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$.* In a Dirichlet column, $C_{jj}$ is independent of how the rest of the column, $1-C_{jj}$, is shared among the wrong classes, and that share follows $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha_{ij},\,i\ne j)$, here with all parameters equal to $\beta=\hat\nu(1-\hat m)/(K-1)$. A small $\beta$ lets the mistakes concentrate on the few classes that $V$ shows to be confused, while the uniform prior adds one imaginary mistake to every wrong class, which flattens the column (illustrative: with $104$ instances, $99$ correct and $5$ mistakes on $2$ of $25$ wrong classes, $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ gives the $23$ never-confused classes a total of $23/130\approx0.18$ of the column). Choosing $\beta$ independently of $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ would leave the Dirichlet family and change BayesianCC.
- *Small concentrations.* For an accurate classifier $\beta<1$ (illustrative: $\hat\nu=41$, $\hat m=0.95$ give $\beta\approx0.08$); a cell never filled on $V$ then has a posterior density that grows without bound at $0$. In the unconstrained coordinates where NUTS samples, the density stays finite but has a long tail, which can slow the sampler down. This is why E2.4 records the NUTS diagnostics.

**What happens to the shift.** With the uniform prior every recall above $1/K$ is pulled down, and the pulls add up in $A=\sum_j\pi_jr_j$. With the empirical-Bayes prior the bias of class $\omega_j$ is $\frac{\hat\nu}{n_j+\hat\nu}(\hat m-r_j)$: positive for the classes below the typical recall and negative for those above it. Under the model, $\mathbb E[m-r_j]=0$, so the biases cancel on accuracy on average over the classes; under UPP the prevalences are drawn independently of the classifier, so this holds on average over the bags. Two limits remain. The classes may not be exchangeable: a truly hard class is pulled up towards the others, and bags dominated by it are displaced (pooling reduces the total squared error over the classes, not the error of every class). And causes 2 and 4 of E2.2 are not addressed.

**Using $V$ twice.** $V$ chooses $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ and then updates $C$ again, while the posterior treats $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ as known constants. A fully Bayesian model would average over them, $p(r_j\mid V)=\int p(r_j\mid V,m,\nu)\,p(m,\nu\mid V)\,dm\,d\nu$, and by the law of total variance

$$
\operatorname{Var}(r_j\mid V)=\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(r_j\mid V,m,\nu)\mid V\big]}_{\text{kept by empirical Bayes}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb E[r_j\mid V,m,\nu]\mid V\big)}_{\text{left out}},
$$

where the left-out term is about $(1-w_j)^2\operatorname{Var}(\hat m)$ if the uncertainty on $\nu$ is ignored. The error of $\hat m$ is shared by all the classes, so on accuracy it does not average out: the left-out standard deviation of the accuracy is about $\sum_j\pi_j(1-w_j)\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$. $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$ is estimated from the curvature of $\ell$ at its maximum. For one parameter $\theta$, the log-likelihood near its maximum is close to a parabola, $\ell(\theta)\approx\ell(\hat\theta)+\frac12\ell''(\hat\theta)(\theta-\hat\theta)^2$, which is the logarithm of a normal density with variance $-1/\ell''(\hat\theta)$: a sharply peaked likelihood (large $-\ell''$) means a small uncertainty, and $\operatorname{sd}(\hat\theta)\approx1/\sqrt{-\ell''(\hat\theta)}$. With two parameters $\theta=(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ the expansion at the maximum is $\ell(\theta)\approx\ell(\hat\theta)-\frac12(\theta-\hat\theta)^\top H(\theta-\hat\theta)$, where $H$ is minus the matrix of second derivatives of $\ell$ at $\hat\theta$ (minus the Hessian), the *observed information*; exponentiating gives the shape of a bivariate normal density $\exp(-\frac12x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x)$ with $\Sigma=H^{-1}$. The covariance matrix of a multivariate normal holds the variance of each coordinate on its diagonal and the covariances off it, so the variances of the estimates are the diagonal of $H^{-1}$. "The variances" are those of the estimates $\hat\theta_1,\hat\theta_2$: how much they would change over the validation sets we could have drawn or, in Bayesian terms, how uncertain $\theta$ is after $V$ (with a flat prior the posterior is approximately this normal, the Laplace approximation; in frequentist terms the maximum-likelihood estimate is approximately normal with this covariance). The inverse, and not $1/H_{11}$, because for a $2\times2$ matrix

$$
(H^{-1})_{11}=\frac{H_{22}}{H_{11}H_{22}-H_{12}^2}=\frac1{H_{11}-H_{12}^2/H_{22}}\ \ge\ \frac1{H_{11}} :
$$

$1/H_{11}$ is the variance of $\theta_1$ if $\theta_2$ were known (the curvature along $\theta_1$ alone), while $(H^{-1})_{11}$ is its variance when $\theta_2$ is unknown as well; when the two are correlated ($H_{12}\ne0$), part of the uncertainty on $\theta_2$ leaks into $\theta_1$. E2.4 computes $H$ in the coordinates of the optimiser, $\theta_1=\operatorname{logit}m$ and $\theta_2=\ln\nu$, which are unconstrained and in which the likelihood is closer to a parabola, by central finite differences of the analytic gradient; the inverse gives $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{logit}\hat m)$, which is then converted to $m$. When $\hat\nu$ is at its bound the likelihood is flat in $\nu$, $H_{22}\approx0$ and $H$ cannot be inverted; the code then treats $\nu$ as fixed and uses $1/H_{11}$, the conditional variance, which by the inequality above underestimates $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$ somewhat, on the pairs where it matters most. The standard deviation of $\hat m$ then follows by the delta method: if $\hat m=g(\hat\theta)$ with $\hat\theta$ close to the truth, a first-order expansion $g(\hat\theta)\approx g(\theta)+g'(\theta)(\hat\theta-\theta)$ gives $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)\approx\lvert g'(\hat\theta)\rvert\operatorname{sd}(\hat\theta)$; for $m=1/(1+e^{-\theta})$, the inverse of the logit, $g'(\theta)=m(1-m)$, so $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)=\hat m(1-\hat m)\operatorname{sd}(\operatorname{logit}\hat m)$. In an illustrative case like `isolet` ($K=26$, $n_j=104$, $\hat\nu=41$, spread of the recalls $0.04$) it is about $0.002$, against about $0.0036$ for the standard deviation that the uncertainty on $C$ gives the accuracy, so the $C$ part of the width would be about $13\%$ too small; the uncertainty on $\pi^Q$ makes the effect on the whole interval smaller. Because $V$ is drawn once, the error of $\hat m$ is the same in every bag of a pair: if it matters, it appears as a small displacement, not as random width. A second, smaller effect: each class enters $\hat m$, so the centre it is pulled towards has been moved slightly towards itself, and the pull is a little too weak; the effect is of order $1/K$. E2.4 reports the left-out standard deviation per pair. The fully Bayesian version, which samples $(m,\nu)$ with NUTS, would change the model of BayesianCC and is not pursued.

**What the check can and cannot predict.** The check of E2.4 computes $\tilde r_j$ from $V$ alone and compares it with the realised recalls of each bag. It predicts the recall term of the shift, not the coverage (it has no interval), and it leaves out the step in which the target bag updates $C$, the effect of the mistakes on $\hat\pi$ (the prevalence term), and the left-out variance. The variant measures all of these, and the comparison of the two tells which of them matter.

## T.10 Why the empirical-Bayes prior fails with two classes (E2.4, E2.5)

*Intuition:* to learn how much the recalls of a classifier differ from class to class, one needs several classes. With two classes there is a single difference between two recalls, and on validation sets of a few hundred instances it is mostly noise; the fit then either declares the two recalls identical, and fixes both at their common value, or pulls two recalls that differ for a good reason towards each other.

The numerical examples of this section are illustrative, computed by hand, not results; the toy results are in Appendix A.2. We use the notation of §T.9 ($n_j=n^s_j$, $f_j=f_{jj}$, $r_j=C_{jj}$, $\hat r_j=f_j/n_j$), and write $\sigma_j^2=r_j(1-r_j)/n_j$ for the binomial noise of $\hat r_j$. With $K=2$ a column of $C$ is $(r_j,1-r_j)$, the Dirichlet prior on it is a Beta prior on $r_j$, and the two recalls are the true-positive rate (for $\omega_1$, say) and the true-negative rate.

**What the fit has to separate.** By the law of total variance (§T.9), each plain recall varies around $m$ with

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat r_j)=\underbrace{\mathbb E[\sigma_j^2]}_{\text{binomial noise}}+\underbrace{\tau^2}_{\text{true spread}},\qquad \tau^2=\operatorname{Var}(r_j)=\frac{m(1-m)}{\nu+1}.
$$

A large $\nu$ means a small true spread. The observed recalls are scattered for two reasons: the true recalls differ (this is $\tau^2$, which carries $\nu$), and each is measured with noise ($\sigma_j^2$). The fit has to tell the two apart. $V$ shows only how far apart the $\hat r_j$ are, which contains both terms; the noise term is known from $n_j$ and $\hat r_j$, so the information on $\nu$ is the excess of the observed spread over the noise. To see it in the simplest case, take all $n_j$ equal, so that all $\sigma_j$ are equal to some $\sigma$, and measure the spread by the sample variance $s^2=\frac1{K-1}\sum_j(\hat r_j-\bar r)^2$, with $\bar r$ the mean of the $\hat r_j$. The sample variance of independent variables with a common variance is an unbiased estimate of that variance, so $\mathbb E[s^2]=\sigma^2+\tau^2$, and the method of moments estimates the spread by $\hat\tau^2=s^2-\hat\sigma^2$. Illustrative, with $n_j=141$ and $\sigma\approx0.032$, so $\sigma^2\approx0.001$: two recalls $0.10$ apart give $s^2=0.10^2/2=0.005$ (see below for $s^2$ with two classes), $\hat\tau^2\approx0.004$ and $\hat\nu\approx0.14/0.004-1\approx34$, a real spread; two recalls $0.03$ apart give $s^2=0.00045<\sigma^2$: they are closer than noise alone would put them, and no spread can be detected. When the recalls are no further apart than noise explains, $s^2\le\hat\sigma^2$, the estimated spread is zero or negative; a variance cannot be negative, the closest admissible value is $\tau^2=0$, and by the map $\nu=m(1-m)/\tau^2-1$ of §T.9 this is $\hat\nu=\infty$. Maximum likelihood reaches the same conclusion by another route (§T.9, the limit cases): when the counts are less scattered than binomial noise implies, every increase of $\nu$ makes them more probable, so the likelihood keeps increasing with $\nu$ and its maximum is the largest value allowed, the bound $10^4$. The condition $s^2<\sigma^2$ is the moment version; the condition for maximum likelihood is close to it but not identical.

**With two classes, one difference decides.** With $K=2$, $\bar r=(\hat r_1+\hat r_2)/2$, so $\hat r_1-\bar r=d/2$ and $\hat r_2-\bar r=-d/2$ with $d=\hat r_1-\hat r_2$, and

$$
s^2=\frac{(d/2)^2+(d/2)^2}{2-1}=\frac{d^2}{2}.
$$

Approximate each $\hat r_j$ as normal, $\hat r_j\approx\mathcal N(m,\sigma^2+\tau^2)$, independently across the two classes. The difference of two independent normal variables is normal, with the difference of the means and the sum of the variances, so $d\approx\mathcal N(0,2(\sigma^2+\tau^2))$, and $Z=d/\sqrt{2(\sigma^2+\tau^2)}$ is a standard normal variable. The fit reaches the bound when $s^2<\sigma^2$, that is when

$$
Z^2=\frac{d^2}{2(\sigma^2+\tau^2)}=\frac{s^2}{\sigma^2+\tau^2}<\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+\tau^2}=c,\qquad P(\hat\nu=\infty)=P\big(\lvert Z\rvert<\sqrt c\,\big).
$$

**Why $Z$, and the two "1"s.** $Z$ is simply $d$ measured in units of its own standard deviation. Standardising turns any normal variable into a *standard* normal, whose probabilities are known ($P(\lvert Z\rvert<1)\approx0.68$, the familiar "68% within one standard deviation"), so the event $s^2<\sigma^2$, rewritten as $Z^2<c$, can be read off the standard normal. $Z^2$, the square of one standard normal, has by definition the $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom, $\chi^2_1$, so $P(\hat\nu=\infty)=P(\chi^2_1<c)$. Two different "1"s appear below: the subscript of $\chi^2_1$ is the number of degrees of freedom, and the threshold $1$ in $P(\chi^2_1<1)$ is the value of $c$ when the two recalls are truly equal ($\tau=0$, so $c=\sigma^2/\sigma^2=1$); when they differ, $c<1$. More precisely, it is the *statistic* $Z^2$ that has the $\chi^2_1$ distribution; the *event* "the fit reaches the bound" is approximately the event $Z^2<c$, so its probability is the $\chi^2_1$ distribution function evaluated at $c$, $P(\text{bound})\approx P(\chi^2_1<c)=P(\lvert Z\rvert<\sqrt c)$.

- *Equal recalls* ($\tau=0$, $c=1$): $P(\lvert Z\rvert<1)\approx0.68$. Two times out of three the fit declares the recalls equal; here this is correct, and the problem is what the prior does next (below).
- *Different recalls:* with $r_j\approx0.83$ and $n_j=141$, as on `mammographic`, $\sigma=\sqrt{0.83\cdot0.17/141}\approx0.032$; a true spread $\tau=0.03$ (recalls a few points apart) gives $c=0.032^2/(0.032^2+0.03^2)\approx0.53$ and $P(\lvert Z\rvert<0.73)\approx0.53$. Half the time the fit still declares the two recalls identical.

**Why one degree of freedom, although the recalls are independent.** It is precisely because the two recalls are independent that we can count their independent pieces of information: there are two, but only one of them speaks about the spread. Rewrite the pair $(\hat r_1,\hat r_2)$ as its sum and its difference,

$$
u=\hat r_1+\hat r_2,\qquad d=\hat r_1-\hat r_2 .
$$

This is just a rotation of coordinates: knowing $(u,d)$ is the same as knowing $(\hat r_1,\hat r_2)$. With independent variables of equal variance $v$, $\operatorname{Cov}(u,d)=\operatorname{Var}(\hat r_1)-\operatorname{Var}(\hat r_2)=v-v=0$, and for jointly normal variables zero covariance means independence. The two independent measurements therefore become two independent pieces of information:

- $u$ tells where the recalls are, i.e. about $m$; the fit spends it on estimating the centre, $\hat m\approx u/2$. It says nothing about how far apart the recalls are: shifting both recalls by the same amount changes $u$ and leaves $d$ unchanged.
- $d$ tells how far apart they are, i.e. about $\tau^2$, hence about $\nu$; the spread $s^2=d^2/2$ is a function of $d$ alone. Both recalls have mean $m$, so $d$ has mean $0$ and $\mathbb E[d^2]=\operatorname{Var}(d)=2(\sigma^2+\tau^2)$: $d^2$ estimates $2(\sigma^2+\tau^2)$, subtracting the known noise $2\sigma^2$ leaves $2\tau^2$, and $\tau^2$ maps one-to-one onto $\nu$. The sum $u$ is centred on $2m$ and says nothing about the spread.

The whole estimate of $\nu$ rests on one normal variable, and its square is $\chi^2_1$. Degrees of freedom count the independent pieces of information left for the spread after estimating the centre; independence is what lets us count them, not something that removes them. If $m$ were known in advance, nothing would be spent on it, and the spread around $m$ would have $K$ degrees of freedom, $2$ for $K=2$; even then the bound would be reached with probability $P(\chi^2_2/2<1)=1-e^{-1}\approx0.63$ for equal recalls ($\chi^2_2$ is the exponential distribution with mean $2$). The bound is reached often because two classes give too little information about the spread, not only because one piece goes to $m$.

**With $K$ classes.** The same rotation gives one direction for the mean, spent on $m$, and $K-1$ orthogonal directions of differences, spent on the spread, so $(K-1)s^2/(\sigma^2+\tau^2)$ is approximately $\chi^2_{K-1}$, the distribution of a sum of $K-1$ squared independent standard normals (the property of the sample variance of normal variables), and $P(\hat\nu=\infty)\approx P(\chi^2_{K-1}/(K-1)<c)$. $\chi^2_{K-1}/(K-1)$ is an average of $K-1$ squared standard normals, each with mean $1$, so by the law of large numbers it concentrates around $1$ as $K$ grows, and for $c<1$ the probability goes to $0$. With many classes the fit estimates the spread from many recalls; with two, from one difference. The quantity that decides is the gap statistic $z=d/\sqrt{\hat\sigma_1^2+\hat\sigma_2^2}$, which E2.5 records: the bound is reached when $\lvert z\rvert$ is below about $1$. The toy of Appendix A.2 finds the bound in $84\%$ of its fits with equal recalls and in $56\%$ with recalls $0.80$ and $0.86$: maximum likelihood reaches it somewhat more often than this approximation. The calculation is an approximation in several ways: it uses the moment estimate instead of maximum likelihood, assumes normality, and takes equal $n_j$ and similar recalls; it explains the order of magnitude, and the toy confirms the behaviour.

**At the bound, the plug-in prior removes the uncertainty of the centre.** With $\hat\nu=10^4$ the prior on each recall has standard deviation $\sqrt{\hat m(1-\hat m)/(\hat\nu+1)}\approx\sqrt{0.14/10^4}\approx0.004$, and the weight of $V$ in the posterior mean is $w_j=141/(141+10^4)\approx0.014$: both recalls are fixed at $\hat m$. But $\hat m$ is itself an estimate: at the bound it is the pooled recall $\sum_jf_j/\sum_jn_j$, a frequency over about $282$ instances, with standard deviation about $\sqrt{0.14/282}\approx0.022$. The posterior states $\pm0.004$ where $V$ supports $\pm0.022$. This is the left-out term of §T.9 ("Using $V$ twice"), which at the bound is the whole uncertainty of the recalls, since $1-w_j\approx1$; it is why the left-out standard deviation of E2.4 flags the `mammographic` pairs ($0.020$–$0.023$, the largest of the $75$ pairs). The failure does not need the pooling to be wrong: in the toy, with two recalls that are truly equal, the realised coverage of the empirical-Bayes prior is $0.72$, against $0.95$ for the uniform prior (Appendix A.2). When the recalls are close to $1$, $\hat m(1-\hat m)$ is tiny and so is $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$: this is why `hand_digits` MLP and SVM reach the bound without harm.

**A corrected strength.** The left-out variance can be put back into the prior. This is not part of the optimisation of §T.9: it is a correction applied after the fit, in four steps (a new hypothesis, used only by the toy of Appendix A.2 as `EB-nu*`; `BayCAP` does not have it).

1. *Fit:* obtain $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ by maximising $\ell$ (§T.9).
2. *Measure how uncertain $\hat m$ is:* near its maximum the log-likelihood is close to a parabola, and the sharper the parabola, the better determined the parameter, $\operatorname{sd}(\hat\theta)\approx1/\sqrt{-\ell''(\hat\theta)}$ (§T.9, "Using $V$ twice"). The code computes this in $\operatorname{logit}m$ (when $\hat\nu$ is at its bound, it treats $\nu$ as fixed and uses the curvature in $m$ alone) and converts it to $m$ by the delta method, $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)=\hat m(1-\hat m)\operatorname{sd}(\operatorname{logit}\hat m)$; this is the `sd_m` of the fit, which E2.4 already uses for the left-out standard deviation.
3. *The variance the prior should have:* the plug-in prior acts as if $m$ were known exactly, with variance $\operatorname{Var}(r\mid m)=m(1-m)/(\nu+1)$. If instead $m$ is treated as uncertain, with mean $\hat m$ and standard deviation $s=\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$, the law of total variance (§T.9) gives the variance of a recall before seeing its own class:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(r)=\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(r\mid m)\big]}_{\approx\,\hat m(1-\hat m)/(\hat\nu+1)}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb E[r\mid m]\big)}_{=\operatorname{Var}(m)=s^2},
$$

since $\mathbb E[r\mid m]=m$. The second term is exactly the one the plug-in drops.
4. *A Beta with that variance:* the prior must remain a Beta to fit into the Dirichlet, so we keep its centre $\hat m$ and choose its strength $\nu^*$ so that its variance equals the total of step 3,

$$
\frac{\hat m(1-\hat m)}{\nu^*+1}=\frac{\hat m(1-\hat m)}{\hat\nu+1}+s^2\iff\frac1{\nu^*+1}=\frac1{\hat\nu+1}+\frac{s^2}{\hat m(1-\hat m)},
$$

and build the Dirichlet parameters with $\nu^*$ in place of $\hat\nu$: $\alpha_{jj}=\nu^*\hat m$ and $\alpha_{ij}=\nu^*(1-\hat m)/(K-1)$.

Step 4 is *moment matching*. When $m$ is uncertain, the prior of a recall is the Beta averaged over the plausible values of $m$, $p(r)=\int\operatorname{Beta}(r\mid\nu m,\nu(1-m))\,p(m)\,dm$: a continuous mixture of Betas, each centred at a different $m$. A mixture of Betas is in general not a Beta (mixing two well-separated Betas gives a distribution with two peaks, which no Beta has), but BayCAP needs a Beta to fit into the Dirichlet, so we take the Beta that agrees with the mixture on its first two moments: its mean, $\mathbb E[r]=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[r\mid m]\big]=\mathbb E[m]=\hat m$ by the tower property, and its variance, from the law of total variance in step 3. A Beta is fixed uniquely by its mean and variance ($m$, and $\nu=m(1-m)/\operatorname{Var}-1$), so this determines $\nu^*$. The approximation is good when the mixture has one peak and is not very skewed, as here, where $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$ is small compared with the distance of $\hat m$ from $0$ and $1$. It also ignores the uncertainty on $\nu$: the hierarchical model of §T.12 averages over both $m$ and $\nu$, with weights $p(m,\nu\mid V)$, while $\nu^*$ fixes $\nu$ at $\hat\nu$ and averages over $m$ only. At the bound this is the larger omission, since $V$ barely constrains $\nu$ and smaller values, which give wider priors, remain plausible; including them would widen the prior further, so $\nu^*$ is, if anything, still too large. $\nu^*$ is therefore only the leading correction to the plug-in prior, the variance of $\hat m$ from a normal approximation, not the full hierarchical answer: it drops the shape of the mixture beyond its first two moments, the uncertainty on $\nu$ and the correlation between $\hat m$ and $\hat\nu$; it keeps the Dirichlet family. With $\hat\nu=10^4$, $\hat m=0.83$ and $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)=0.022$, the second term is $0.022^2/0.141\approx0.0034$ and dominates the first ($10^{-4}$), so $\nu^*\approx280$: the prior is worth about as many instances as the validation data from which $\hat m$ was estimated (about $282$), instead of $10^4$ imaginary ones. In the toy it raises the realised coverage at the bound from $0.72$ to $0.88$, with the Winkler score of the uniform prior ($0.130$ against $0.126$), but not lower (Appendix A.2).

**What ignoring the uncertainty on $\nu$ means.** The fully Bayesian prior on a recall averages over everything that is unknown about the "factory",

$$
p(r\mid V)=\iint\operatorname{Beta}\big(r\mid\nu m,\nu(1-m)\big)\,p(m,\nu\mid V)\,dm\,d\nu,
$$

where $p(m,\nu\mid V)$ says which pairs $(m,\nu)$ are plausible after seeing the counts. $\nu^*$ keeps the uncertainty on $m$, through $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$, but puts all the weight of $\nu$ on the single value $\hat\nu$, as if $\nu$ were known exactly. By the law of total variance (§T.9), conditioning on both $m$ and $\nu$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(r)=\mathbb E_{m,\nu}\Big[\frac{m(1-m)}{\nu+1}\Big]+\operatorname{Var}(m).
$$

$\nu^*$ computes the first term at $\nu=\hat\nu$ only. If several values of $\nu$ are plausible, the first term is the average of $m(1-m)/(\nu+1)$ over them, and since $1/(\nu+1)$ is much larger for small $\nu$ than for large $\nu$, the average is dominated by the smaller plausible values and can be far larger than its value at $\hat\nu$. How much this matters depends on how sharply $V$ determines $\nu$. With many classes (e.g. `isolet`) the likelihood has a clear peak in $\nu$, $p(\nu\mid V)$ is narrow, and fixing $\nu=\hat\nu$ loses little. With two classes at the bound (e.g. `mammographic`) the likelihood is flat for large $\nu$ (§T.12): beyond some point, $\nu=300$, $1000$ and $10^4$ explain the two counts almost equally well, and the optimiser lands on $10^4$ only because it is the largest value allowed, not because $V$ prefers it. So $V$ does not rule out the smaller values, and they give much wider priors (illustrative, $\hat m=0.83$, $m(1-m)\approx0.14$):

| $\nu$ | prior standard deviation $\sqrt{0.14/(\nu+1)}$ |
|---|---|
| $10^4$ | $0.004$ |
| $1000$ | $0.012$ |
| $300$ | $0.022$ |
| $100$ | $0.037$ |

If all values between $100$ and $10^4$ were equally plausible on a log scale, the average of $1/(\nu+1)$ over that range would be about $(1/100-1/10^4)/\ln100\approx0.0022$, so the first term would be about $0.14\cdot0.0022\approx0.0003$, a standard deviation of about $0.017$, against $0.004$ at $\hat\nu=10^4$: the omitted part is as large as the $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)\approx0.022$ that $\nu^*$ adds back (illustrative). Including the uncertainty on $\nu$ would raise the prior variance, and a larger variance means a smaller strength ($\nu=m(1-m)/\operatorname{Var}-1$), so the fully Bayesian prior would be weaker than the Beta with strength $\nu^*\approx280$, and its intervals wider: $\nu^*$ corrects in the right direction but not far enough. Two further facts point the same way: at the bound the code computes $\operatorname{sd}(\hat m)$ with $\nu$ fixed, which slightly underestimates it (§T.9, "Using $V$ twice"), and in the toy `EB-nu*` covers $0.88$ rather than $0.95$, consistent with a prior that is still too narrow, though not a proof. The caveat: since the likelihood is flat in $\nu$ at the bound, how much wider the full answer is depends on the prior put on $\nu$, not on $V$ (§T.12); with the log-uniform range above it roughly doubles the variance, with a prior that favours large $\nu$ it would add less. The direction is certain, the size is not determined by the data, for the same reason that the hierarchical model gives no data-driven answer at $K=2$.

**With a moderate $\hat\nu$, the two recalls are pulled together.** The empirical-Bayes model assumes that the recalls of the classes are exchangeable, draws from a common distribution with no reason to expect one class to be harder than another (§T.9). With many classes (letters, digits) this is a fair approximation. With two classes the recalls are the true-positive and the true-negative rates, and they differ for a structural reason: the decision threshold of $h$ trades one for the other, and the class balance of $L$ moves the threshold towards the majority class. When they differ clearly, $\hat\nu$ is finite and the prior pulls both towards their mean. The prior part of the recall term (§T.5) is

$$
R_0=\sum_j\pi_j(\tilde r_j-\hat r_j)=\sum_j\pi_j(1-w_j)(\hat m-\hat r_j),
$$

using $\tilde r_j=w_j\hat r_j+(1-w_j)\hat m$ (§T.9). With equal weights $w_j=w$ and $\hat m\approx(\hat r_1+\hat r_2)/2$, we have $\hat m-\hat r_1=(\hat r_2-\hat r_1)/2$ and $\hat m-\hat r_2=-(\hat r_2-\hat r_1)/2$, so

$$
R_0\approx(1-w)\,\frac{(\pi_1-\pi_2)(\hat r_2-\hat r_1)}{2}.
$$

The bias is zero at $\pi=(1/2,1/2)$ and largest at extreme prevalences, which UPP draws often; it grows with the gap between the two recalls; and it is not noise that $V$ corrects, since pooling treats a real gap as noise. Illustrative: $n_j=141$, $\hat\nu=10$ ($1-w=10/151\approx0.066$) and recalls $0.70$ and $0.90$ give $\lvert R_0\rvert$ up to $0.066\cdot0.2/2\approx0.007$ for the most extreme bags. The uniform prior also pulls, but towards $1/2$ and with $2$ imaginary instances: $R_0=\sum_j\pi_j\frac{2}{n_j+2}(\frac12-\hat r_j)$, about $-0.004$ here. In the toy, with recalls $0.70$ and $0.90$, the median $\hat\nu$ is $17$ and the realised coverage falls from $0.95$ to $0.91$ (Appendix A.2); on `german` the pull adds to a prevalence term of constant sign (E2.2) and the coverage loss is larger.

**With a fitted $\hat\nu$ the pull does not grow with the gap** (added after E2.5). The formula above holds $\hat\nu$ fixed, but $\hat\nu$ is fitted on the same two recalls, and it falls as they move apart. With the method of moments of this section, the spread of two recalls $d=\lvert\hat r_1-\hat r_2\rvert$ apart is $s^2=d^2/2$ (each is $d/2$ from their mean, and $2\cdot(d/2)^2=d^2/2$); when $d$ is well above the noise, $\hat\tau^2=s^2-\hat\sigma^2\approx d^2/2$, and the strength is

$$
\hat\nu=\frac{\hat m(1-\hat m)}{\hat\tau^2}-1\approx\frac{2\hat m(1-\hat m)}{d^2}.
$$

When $\hat\nu$ is much smaller than $n_j$, the weight of the prior is $1-w_j=\hat\nu/(n_j+\hat\nu)\approx\hat\nu/n_j$, and each recall is moved by

$$
(1-w_j)\,\lvert\hat m-\hat r_j\rvert\approx\frac{\hat\nu}{n_j}\cdot\frac d2\approx\frac{\hat m(1-\hat m)}{n_j\,d},
$$

which *decreases* as $d$ grows: a larger gap makes a weaker prior, and the weaker prior pulls less. Illustrative, with $\hat m=0.75$ and $n_j=141$: $d=0.2$ gives $\hat\nu\approx9$ and a move of about $0.007$; $d=0.5$ gives $\hat\nu\approx1.5$ and about $0.003$ (maximum likelihood gives somewhat different values than the method of moments, but the same trend). Moreover the effect on accuracy, $R_0\approx(1-w)(\pi_1-\pi_2)(\hat r_2-\hat r_1)/2$, has the sign of $\pi_1-\pi_2$; UPP draws $\pi_1$ uniformly, so $\pi_1-\pi_2$ has mean $0$ over the bags, and the mean of $R_0$ over the bags is close to $0$ (it is not exactly $0$ only because $w_1\ne w_2$ when $n_1\ne n_2$). E2.5 finds both: $\lvert\Delta R\rvert$ falls as the gap grows, and the mean pull is at most $0.006$ on the pairs with pools of at least $100$ instances. With a finite $\hat\nu$ the EB prior harms instead through its strength: $\hat\nu$ imaginary instances make the interval narrower, which costs coverage on pairs whose interval is already displaced for another reason (E2.5, Analysis).

**There was little to gain.** With $K=2$ the uniform prior is $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$, two imaginary instances per column, and its prior part is small (E2.2: $R_0$ between $-0.006$ and $-0.001$ on the binary pairs); in the toy with bags drawn from the population its realised coverage is $0.94$–$0.95$. The pull towards $1/K$ that the empirical-Bayes prior removes is a large-$K$ problem; at $K=2$ the empirical-Bayes prior can gain little and its failures cost coverage in full. This holds when $n_j$ is in the hundreds: the uniform prior part is $R_0=\sum_j\pi_j\frac{1-2\hat r_j}{n_j+2}$, which for $\hat r_j=0.95$ is about $-0.006$ with $n_j=141$ but about $-0.041$ with $n_j=20$; E2.5 finds the largest gains of the EB prior on such small validation sets (`wine.x`, `iris.2`).

**Possible remedies** (new hypotheses, not decided): (a) the corrected strength $\nu^*$, which repairs the collapse at the bound but not the pull; (b) the uniform prior when $K$ is small, at least for $K=2$, since pooling needs several exchangeable classes; the $\chi^2_{K-1}$ argument above suggests how the smallest $K$ for the empirical-Bayes prior could be chosen. Neither addresses cause 4 (§T.13).

## T.11 Concentrations below 1 and the sampler (E2.4)

*Intuition:* a Dirichlet parameter below $1$ says "this entry is probably almost exactly zero", with most of its mass squeezed against the edge of the simplex. NUTS has to explore that edge with the same steps it uses for the entries that $V$ fixes precisely, and it fails.

**How much mass sits near zero.** By aggregation (§T.9), one entry of a $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$ column is $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_{ij},\nu-\alpha_{ij})$, with density proportional to $x^{\alpha_{ij}-1}(1-x)^{\nu-\alpha_{ij}-1}$; for $\alpha_{ij}<1$ it grows without bound as $x\to0$. Near $0$, $(1-x)^{\nu-\alpha_{ij}-1}\approx1$, so with $a=\alpha_{ij}$ and $b=\nu-\alpha_{ij}$

$$
P(x<\varepsilon)\approx\frac1{B(a,b)}\int_0^\varepsilon x^{a-1}\,dx=\frac{\varepsilon^a}{a\,B(a,b)}.
$$

For a small $a$, $B(a,b)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)\approx1/a$, because $\Gamma(a)\approx1/a$ and $\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)\approx1$; hence $P(x<\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon^a$. With $a=0.05$, $P(x<10^{-6})\approx10^{-0.3}\approx0.5$: half the prior mass of the entry is below one in a million. After $V$ the posterior of the column is $\operatorname{Dir}(f_{\cdot j}+\alpha_{\cdot j})$, so the cells that $V$ fills get parameters above $1$, and the cells it never fills keep $\alpha_{ij}<1$. For an accurate classifier with many classes, most of the off-diagonal cells are never filled (illustrative, as in §T.9: $5$ mistakes over $25$ wrong classes).

**What the sampler sees.** NUTS moves in unconstrained coordinates; for a small entry they behave like its logarithm. If $x$ has density $p_x(x)\propto x^{a-1}$ near $0$, then $y=\ln x$ has density $p_y(y)=p_x(e^y)\,e^y\propto e^{(a-1)y}e^y=e^{ay}$ (change of variables, with $dx/dy=e^y$): an exponential tail towards $-\infty$ with scale $1/a$, $20$ log-units for $a=0.05$. The filled entries, meanwhile, are fixed by $V$ within a few hundredths. A step size that suits the filled entries crawls along the tail, and one that suits the tail overshoots where the tail meets the region constrained by the data, where the curvature changes abruptly; the simulated trajectory then loses energy conservation, which NUTS reports as a divergent transition. This agrees with E2.4: no pair whose smallest concentration is at least $1$ has a divergence, and every pair with divergences in most bags has it below $0.17$. Why `isolet` LR and kNN give a NaN split-$\hat R$ is not explained; a guess, not checked, is that some entries underflow (the smallest normal single-precision number is about $10^{-38}$) and stay constant across draws.

**Raising the concentrations would undo the prior.** Raising every off-diagonal parameter to $1$ removes the singularity but, by aggregation, the wrong classes of the column then have total parameter $K-1$, and the recall gets $\operatorname{Beta}(\hat\nu\hat m,K-1)$ instead of $\operatorname{Beta}(\hat\nu\hat m,\hat\nu(1-\hat m))$. Illustrative, as in §T.9 for `isolet` ($\hat\nu=41$, $\hat m=0.95$, $K=26$): $\operatorname{Beta}(39,25)$, with mean $39/64\approx0.61$; the pull towards a low recall that the empirical-Bayes prior removed comes back.

**Separating the recall from the share of the mistakes.** A Dirichlet column has the *neutrality* property: the recall $C_{jj}$ is independent of how the remaining mass $1-C_{jj}$ is shared among the wrong classes, $s_j=C_{-j,j}/(1-C_{jj})$, and $s_j\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha_{ij},i\ne j)$ (it follows from the Gamma construction of §T.9: $s_j$ normalises the Gamma variables of the wrong classes among themselves, and the normalised vector is independent of their sum). The prior is therefore a Beta on the recall times a Dirichlet on the share of the mistakes, and the empirical-Bayes prior sets both from $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$. A prior that keeps $C_{jj}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\hat\nu\hat m,\hat\nu(1-\hat m))$ and gives the share $s_j\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1_{K-1})$ would keep the pull towards $\hat m$ and have no parameter below $1$ off the diagonal. It is no longer a Dirichlet on the column (unless $\hat\nu(1-\hat m)=K-1$), so it changes the prior of BayesianCC, not its likelihoods. Separately, running NUTS in double precision and with several chains would show whether the NaN diagnostics are numerical; this is a diagnostic, not a fix. Both are new hypotheses, not decided. On `poker_hand` and `shuttle`, where $\hat\nu<1$, the diagonal parameter is below $1$ as well, and only a different prior on the recall would remove it.

## T.12 A prior per class, and a hierarchical prior

*Intuition:* the empirical-Bayes prior learns one typical recall from all the classes. Giving each class its own prior removes what the classes teach each other; treating the typical recall as unknown instead of fixed keeps it and adds the uncertainty that the plug-in prior drops.

**A Beta prior per class, fitted on $V$.** If class $\omega_j$ has its own $(m_j,\nu_j)$, fitted to its own count, its marginal likelihood is the average of the binomial probability of $f_j$ over the prior, $P(f_j\mid m_j,\nu_j)=\int\binom{n_j}{f_j}r^{f_j}(1-r)^{n_j-f_j}p(r)\,dr$. An average cannot exceed the largest value it averages, and the binomial probability is largest at $r=\hat r_j$ (§T.9), so the marginal likelihood is at most $\binom{n_j}{f_j}\hat r_j^{f_j}(1-\hat r_j)^{n_j-f_j}$, reached when all the prior mass is at $\hat r_j$: $\hat m_j=\hat r_j$ and $\hat\nu_j\to\infty$. The fitted prior says that each recall is exactly its plain value, BayCAP then uses $V$ a second time on top of it, and the uncertainty on $C$ disappears: the collapse of §T.10 on every class, with no pooling. A prior per class is useful only if its centre comes from parameters shared by the classes and from a known property of each class, for example $\operatorname{logit}m_j=a+b\ln n^L_j$ with a common $\nu$; that might help a class whose recall differs from the others (`nursery` MLP in E2.4, if its hard class is also rare in $L$, a guess not checked), but not with $K=2$, where two counts cannot fix three parameters, nor with concentrations below $1$, which come from the share of the mistakes.

**A hierarchical prior.** Here $(m,\nu)$ get a prior of their own and NUTS samples them with $C$ and $\pi^Q$. E2.6 screens this prior at no cost (§T.14). The posterior of a recall then averages over $(m,\nu)$, and the left-out term of §T.9 is included by construction; $\nu^*$ of §T.10 is its first-order approximation. With $K=2$ the result depends on the prior on $\nu$. As $\nu\to\infty$ the beta-binomial tends to $\operatorname{Binomial}(n_j,m)$, so the likelihood of $(m,\nu)$ levels off at the positive value $\prod_j\binom{n_j}{f_j}m^{f_j}(1-m)^{n_j-f_j}$ instead of falling to $0$, and for large $\nu$ the posterior of $\nu$ is proportional to its prior. With a flat prior on $\ln\nu$, the usual choice for a scale, the posterior cannot be normalised; a proper prior that decays is needed, such as a uniform prior on $(m,1/\sqrt\nu)$ (Gelman et al., *Bayesian Data Analysis*), which by the change of variables $u=\nu^{-1/2}$, $\lvert du/d\nu\rvert=\frac12\nu^{-3/2}$, is $p(m,\nu)\propto\nu^{-3/2}$. With two classes $V$ barely constrains $\nu$, so the width of the interval is decided mostly by this prior. It does not remove the pull of §T.10, since the model still treats the two recalls as exchangeable. And it makes the sampling harder: when $\nu$ is large every recall must be close to $m$, when it is small they can spread, so $(\ln\nu,r_1,\dots,r_K)$ form a narrow neck opening into a wide region (Neal's funnel), the textbook source of divergences in hierarchical models; the standard cure, $\operatorname{logit}r_j=\mu+\sigma z_j$ with $z_j\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ sampled instead of $r_j$, leaves the Dirichlet family. Both change the model of BayesianCC, and the hierarchical version was set aside by the user on 2026-10-26 (Appendix C). A toy with known prevalences (Appendix A.4) computes the hierarchical posterior on a grid: it covers about $0.95$ at every $K$ from $2$ to $26$ with priors on $\nu$ that do not favour large values, gains most over $\nu^*$ and over the uniform prior at $K=3$ and $K=7$, is no better than the uniform prior at $K=2$, and does not test the sampling problems above.

## T.13 A finite test pool (cause 4)

*Intuition:* the $100$ bags of a pair are not $100$ fresh samples of the target distribution: they are re-drawn from the same few hundred test instances. Whatever is peculiar about those instances is repeated in every bag, and BayCAP, which knows nothing about the pool, cannot allow for it.

**How `main` builds the bags.** `quapy`'s `fetch_UCIBinaryDataset` sets aside $30\%$ of a dataset as the test pool $U$ (`test_split=0.3`), and `cap-ml` splits the rest in half into $L$ and $V$. Each bag draws its prevalence with UPP and takes $\operatorname{round}(N^Q\pi_j)$ instances of class $\omega_j$ from $U$, with replacement (`LabelledCollection.sampling_index`). On `mammographic` $U$ has $249$ instances, so a bag of $1000$ uses each about four times, and all bags are made of the same $249$ instances.

**What the bags measure.** Let $P_j$ be the number of instances of class $\omega_j$ in $U$ and $g_j$ the share of them that $h$ classifies correctly, the recall of $h$ on the pool. Drawing with replacement, each instance of class $\omega_j$ in a bag is a correct one with probability $g_j$, independently of the others, so a bag has $\operatorname{Binomial}(n^Q_j,g_j)$ correct instances of class $\omega_j$, not $\operatorname{Binomial}(n^Q_j,r_j)$. By linearity of the expectation, the accuracy of the bags varies around the accuracy of the pool,

$$
\mathbb E[A\mid U,\pi^Q]=\sum_j\pi_jg_j,
$$

and not around the population accuracy $\sum_j\pi_jr_j$ that the population table describes.

**Two independent sampling errors.** Each class has three recalls: the population recall $r_j$, its estimate on $V$, $\hat r_j$, and the recall on the pool, $g_j$. $V$ and $U$ are disjoint random samples, so, approximating the binomial frequencies as normal,

$$
\hat r_j-r_j\approx\mathcal N\Big(0,\frac{r_j(1-r_j)}{n_j}\Big),\qquad g_j-r_j\approx\mathcal N\Big(0,\frac{r_j(1-r_j)}{P_j}\Big),\qquad\text{independently}.
$$

BayCAP's posterior on $r_j$ is centred near $\hat r_j$ with a standard deviation of about $\sqrt{r_j(1-r_j)/n_j}$, the uncertainty of $\hat r_j$ around $r_j$. The bags, however, are scored against recalls centred on $g_j$, and by the variance of a difference of independent variables

$$
\hat r_j-g_j=(\hat r_j-r_j)-(g_j-r_j),\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat r_j-g_j)=r_j(1-r_j)\Big(\frac1{n_j}+\frac1{P_j}\Big).
$$

The interval allows for the first term and not for the second, so for what it is scored against it is too narrow by the factor $\sqrt{1+n_j/P_j}$: about $\sqrt{2.1}\approx1.45$ on `mammographic` ($n_j\approx141$, $P_j\approx125$, illustrative). The realised pushforward does not help: it adds the spread of $\operatorname{Binomial}(n^Q_j,g_j)$ around $g_j$, not the error $g_j-r_j$. On accuracy the extra error is $S=\sum_j\pi_j(g_j-r_j)$, with

$$
\operatorname{sd}(S)=\sqrt{\sum_j\pi_j^2\,\frac{r_j(1-r_j)}{P_j}},
$$

illustrative $0.024$ at $\pi=(1/2,1/2)$ and $0.034$ at $\pi=(1,0)$ for $r_j=0.83$ and $P_j=125$; the whole gap between $V$ and the bags, $\sum_j\pi_j(\hat r_j-g_j)$, has standard deviation $0.033$ at $\pi=(1/2,1/2)$.

**Why it displaces the interval.** $g_j$ is fixed once $U$ is drawn, so every bag of a pair inherits the same $g_j-r_j$: the $100$ bags do not average it out, and for this part of the error they amount to one test. The classifiers of a dataset share $U$ (and $V$), so if $U$ happens to contain more hard instances than $V$, all of them do worse on the bags than on $V$, and all get a shift of the same sign. On `mammographic` all five shifts are positive ($+0.034$ to $+0.071$), all in the recall term; a gap of $0.05$ is about $1.5$ standard deviations of $0.033$, unremarkable for one draw shared by correlated classifiers. Since $\operatorname{sd}(S)$ grows as $1/\sqrt{P_j}$, the effect matters only for small pools, which is where E2.2 found the residual recall error.

**What the toy shows** (Appendix A.3). With the uniform prior, the realised coverage falls from $0.96$ for an infinite pool to $0.85$ for a pool of the size of `mammographic` and $0.62$ for $25$ instances per class; a reference interval that adds the pool's own sampling error, $g_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(P_j,r_j)/P_j$, which BayCAP cannot compute since it does not know $P_j$, restores $0.94$–$0.95$ at every pool size. The spread of the pair-level shift matches $\sqrt{\sum_j\frac14r_j(1-r_j)(1/n_j+1/P_j)}$, and the shift follows the gap $\overline{\sum_j\pi_j(\tilde r_j-g_j)}$ with correlation $0.98$–$0.99$. Shifts of the same sign on all the classifiers of a dataset are more frequent than for independent classifiers, but they appear also with an infinite pool, because $V$ is shared as well: they show that one shared sample is atypical, not which one.

**Where it is studied.** The pool term affects every method that learns the rates of $h$ from $V$, not only BayCAP, so it is studied in [[ext4_uncertainty_budget|Extension 4]] (moved there on 2026-10-29): the term $e_P$ of its uncertainty budget (§T.3–§T.4 there), the two readings of cause 4 (a protocol artefact, or a finite-pool estimand), a protocol that draws the bags without replacement and makes each bag a fresh sample (§T.10 there), a check of the pool gap on stored results (E4.c1) and a comparison of the two protocols (E4.c2).

## T.14 The hierarchical prior and its screens (E2.6)

*Intuition:* the empirical-Bayes prior estimates the typical recall $m$ and the spread $1/\nu$ of the classes of a classifier, and then pretends to know them. The hierarchical prior keeps them unknown: it puts a prior on $(m,\nu)$ and lets the data decide how much to believe each value. Its answer for a recall is then an average of the empirical-Bayes answers for every plausible $(m,\nu)$, weighted by how plausible $V$ makes them. Because the target bag barely moves $C$ (E2.4), this average can be computed from $V$ alone, on a grid, without NUTS.

**The model.** For one classifier with $K$ classes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(m,\nu)&\sim p(m)\,p(\nu),\\
r_j=C_{jj}\mid m,\nu&\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\nu m,\nu(1-m)),\quad\text{independently for }j=1,\dots,K,\\
s_j&\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1_{K-1}),\qquad C_{ij}=(1-r_j)\,s_{ij}\ \ (i\ne j),\\
f_{\cdot j}&\sim\operatorname{Mult}(n_j,C_{\cdot j}),\qquad \pi^Q\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1),\qquad n^t\sim\operatorname{Mult}(N^Q,C\pi^Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

The column of $C$ is built in two steps, the recall $r_j$ and the share $s_j$ of the mistakes among the wrong classes (§T.11), so that no concentration below $1$ is forced on the off-diagonal cells. Two properties make the screens simple. First, the number of correct validation instances of class $\omega_j$ is $f_{jj}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_j,r_j)$ whatever $s_j$ is: an instance is correct with probability $r_j$, and how the wrong ones are shared does not change how many are wrong (aggregation property of the multinomial). Second, accuracy is $\sum_j\pi_jr_j$, which does not involve $s_j$. So for the recalls and for accuracy only $(m,\nu)$ and $r_1,\dots,r_K$ matter.

**The posterior of $(m,\nu)$ on a grid.** By Bayes' theorem, $p(m,\nu\mid V)\propto p(m,\nu)\,P(V\mid m,\nu)$. Given $(m,\nu)$ the classes are independent, so $P(V\mid m,\nu)$ is the product over classes of the probability of each count, and the probability of one count is the beta-binomial of §T.9, the binomial probability averaged over the Beta prior of the recall:

$$
p(m,\nu\mid V)\propto p(m)\,p(\nu)\prod_{j=1}^K\binom{n_j}{f_{jj}}\frac{B(\nu m+f_{jj},\,\nu(1-m)+n_j-f_{jj})}{B(\nu m,\,\nu(1-m))}.
$$

On a grid of values $(m_a,\nu_b)$ the right-hand side is computed at every point and divided by its sum, which gives weights $w_{ab}$ that sum to $1$: a discrete approximation of the posterior. The priors on $\nu$ are written as densities in $\ln\nu$, the variable the grid is evenly spaced in. By the change of variables $u=\ln\nu$, $du=d\nu/\nu$, a density $p(\nu)$ becomes $p(\nu)\,\nu$ in $u$: $\nu^{-3/2}$ becomes $\nu^{-1/2}$, the log-uniform $1/\nu$ becomes flat, and the flat $1$ becomes $\nu$. The three priors thus favour, in this order, small, neutral and large values of $\nu$, i.e. wide, neutral and tight pooling. All three are proper only because the grid stops at $\nu=10^4$; for large $\nu$ the likelihood levels off (§T.12), so where $V$ says little the upper bound is part of the prior.

**The posterior of a recall is a mixture.** Given $(m,\nu)$ and $V$, the recall has the conjugate posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(\nu m+f_{jj},\nu(1-m)+n_j-f_{jj})$ (§T.9), with mean and variance

$$
\mu_j(m,\nu)=\frac{f_{jj}+\nu m}{n_j+\nu},\qquad v_j(m,\nu)=\frac{\mu_j(1-\mu_j)}{n_j+\nu+1}.
$$

Averaging over $(m,\nu)$ with the grid weights uses two properties. The law of total expectation, $\mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid G]]$, gives $\mathbb E[r_j\mid V]=\sum_{ab}w_{ab}\,\mu_j(m_a,\nu_b)$. The law of total variance, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid G)]+\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[X\mid G])$, gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(r_j\mid V)=\sum_{ab}w_{ab}\,v_j(m_a,\nu_b)+\sum_{ab}w_{ab}\big(\mu_j(m_a,\nu_b)-\mathbb E[r_j\mid V]\big)^2:
$$

the average uncertainty for a known $(m,\nu)$, plus the uncertainty that comes from not knowing them. The second term is what the plug-in prior drops (§T.10).

**The variance of accuracy from $C$.** For a prevalence $\pi$, accuracy is $A=\sum_j\pi_jr_j$. Given $(m,\nu)$ the recalls are independent, so by the variance of a weighted sum of independent variables $\operatorname{Var}(A\mid m,\nu,V)=\sum_j\pi_j^2v_j(m,\nu)$, and $\mathbb E[A\mid m,\nu,V]=\sum_j\pi_j\mu_j(m,\nu)$. Over $(m,\nu)$ the recalls are no longer independent: they all move with $m$. The law of total variance handles this without computing covariances:

$$
V_C(\pi)=\operatorname{Var}(A\mid V)=\sum_{ab}w_{ab}\sum_j\pi_j^2v_j(m_a,\nu_b)+\sum_{ab}w_{ab}\Big(\sum_j\pi_j\mu_j(m_a,\nu_b)-\mathbb E[A\mid V]\Big)^2.
$$

Under the uniform prior the same quantity is in closed form, since the posterior columns are independent Dirichlet and, by aggregation, $r_j\mid V\sim\operatorname{Beta}(f_{jj}+1,n_j-f_{jj}+K-1)$; under the empirical-Bayes prior, with $(m,\nu)$ fixed at $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$, the second term is $0$.

**D4: predicting a method's draws from those of the uniform prior.** BayCAP's accuracy draws for a bag vary because of $C$, because of $\pi^Q$ and, for the realised pushforward, because of the luck of the bag. Changing the prior on $C$ changes, to first order, only the part due to $C$: E2.4 found that the target bag barely moves $C$, so the posterior of the recalls is the one computed from $V$. Let $a^{(r)}$ be the stored draws with the uniform prior, $c$ their mean and $s^2$ their variance, $\bar\pi$ the posterior-mean prevalence of the bag (from `estim_cts`, no labels), and write $\mathbb E_{\text{u}}$, $V_{C,\text{u}}$ and $\mathbb E_{\text{new}}$, $V_{C,\text{new}}$ for the moments under the uniform and the new prior. The predicted draws are

$$
a^{(r)}_{\text{new}}=c+\Delta+(a^{(r)}-c)\sqrt{\frac{s^2-V_{C,\text{u}}(\bar\pi)+V_{C,\text{new}}(\bar\pi)}{s^2}},\qquad \Delta=\sum_j\bar\pi_j\big(\mathbb E_{\text{new}}[r_j\mid V]-\mathbb E_{\text{u}}[r_j\mid V]\big):
$$

the centre moves by the change of the recalls weighted by the prevalence, and the spread is rescaled so that its variance loses the part due to $C$ under the uniform prior and gains the part under the new prior, while the rest (from $\pi^Q$ and from the bag) is kept. This ignores three effects: the bag's own update of $C$ (and, in the hierarchical model, of $(m,\nu)$), the covariance between $\pi^Q$ and $C$ in the posterior, and any change of shape beyond the variance. Applying it to the empirical-Bayes prior, whose stored results exist, measures how much these effects matter before the prediction is used for the hierarchical prior. If $s^2-V_{C,\text{u}}+V_{C,\text{new}}\le0$ the prediction is undefined for that bag and is reported as such.

**Diagnosing D4: the estimated prevalences.** An estimate of accuracy made from a table is a weighted average of the recalls, $\hat A=\sum_j\hat\pi_j\hat r_j$, where $\hat\pi_j$ is the estimated share of class $\omega_j$ in the bag and $\hat r_j$ its estimated recall. A change of prior can move both factors. D4 moves only the recalls: it keeps the prevalences $\bar\pi$ of the uniform method. For the empirical-Bayes prior this is an approximation that can fail, for the following reason. The uniform prior gives every cell of a column of $C$ the pseudo-count $1$; the stored empirical-Bayes prior gives the diagonal cell $\hat\nu\hat m$ and each off-diagonal cell $\hat\nu(1-\hat m)/(K-1)$ (§T.9), for example $0.5$ with $\hat\nu=20$, $\hat m=0.9$ and $K=5$. So it changes not only how often the classifier is right, but also which wrong class it predicts when it is wrong. BayCAP estimates the prevalences of the bag by asking which $\pi^Q$ explains the predicted counts, $n^t\approx N^QC\pi^Q$; if the off-diagonal cells of $C$ change, the $\pi^Q$ that explains the same $n^t$ changes too, and the more so the worse $C$ is conditioned (§T.7), since a small change of an ill-conditioned matrix gives a large change of the solution. Accuracy then moves through $\hat\pi$, which D4 does not see.

The two effects can be separated exactly on the stored tables. Write $a^{u}=\sum_j\hat\pi^{u}_j\hat r^{u}_j$ and $a^{EB}=\sum_j\hat\pi^{EB}_j\hat r^{EB}_j$ for the accuracy of the posterior-mean tables of the uniform and of the empirical-Bayes method on the same bag. Adding and subtracting $\sum_j\hat\pi^{u}_j\hat r^{EB}_j$, and grouping the terms,

$$
a^{EB}-a^{u}=\underbrace{\sum_j\hat\pi^{u}_j\big(\hat r^{EB}_j-\hat r^{u}_j\big)}_{R}+\underbrace{\sum_j\big(\hat\pi^{EB}_j-\hat\pi^{u}_j\big)\hat r^{EB}_j}_{P}.
$$

$R$ is the change of accuracy due to the recalls alone, at the prevalences of the uniform method: it is what the shift $\Delta$ of D4 predicts from $V$. $P$ is the change due to the prevalences alone, at the recalls of the empirical-Bayes method: D4 sets it to $0$. The split is the one of E2.2 (recall term and prevalence term), applied to two estimates instead of an estimate and the truth. Two diagnostic versions of the D4 prediction then locate a miss. *D4+P* adds the stored $P$ of each bag to $\Delta$: if it matches the stored coverage where D4 does not, the miss is due to the prevalences. *Stored centre* replaces $\Delta$ by the stored $R+P$, which also corrects $\Delta$ for what the bag itself does to the recalls (a difference between $\Delta$ and $R$): if only this version matches, the miss is due to that update. If neither matches, the centre is right and the miss is in the spread or the shape of the draws. Both versions use the stored empirical-Bayes tables, so they explain the misses rather than predict anything.

The diagnosis matters for the hierarchical prior because its model changes only the recalls. Under the uniform prior, given the recall $r_j$, the shares of the mistakes $C_{ij}/(1-r_j)$ ($i\ne j$) are $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1_{K-1})$, by the aggregation and the neutrality of the Dirichlet; the hierarchical model of this section gives them the same $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1_{K-1})$. The off-diagonal cells still change, since $C_{ij}=(1-r_j)s_{ij}$ follows the recall, but the shares no longer get the pseudo-counts $\hat\nu(1-\hat m)/(K-1)$ of the empirical-Bayes prior in place of $1$. If the misses of D4 come from $P$, the validation on the empirical-Bayes prior is therefore pessimistic for the hierarchical prior; how pessimistic is not measured here.

**D1: how much $V$ says about $\nu$.** If $V$ determines $\nu$, the posterior of $\ln\nu$ is much narrower than its prior and the three priors give nearly the same answer; if not, the posterior of $\ln\nu$ is about as wide as the prior and the answer is the prior's. The ratio of the $90\%$ widths of the posterior and of the prior of $\ln\nu$ measures this: near $0$ the data decide, near $1$ the prior does. The argument of §T.10 says when to expect each: the spread of the recalls is estimated from $K$ counts, with about $K-1$ degrees of freedom, so with $K=2$ one difference has to fix it. The predicted interval (D4) under the three priors then shows how much the choice matters for coverage.

**D2: are the classes alike?** The pooled prior assumes that each recall is a draw from the same $\operatorname{Beta}(\nu m,\nu(1-m))$. A direct check is to fit the pooled prior without class $j$ and ask how surprising its count is. With the weights $w^{(-j)}_{ab}$ of the grid posterior computed from the other classes, the predictive probability of a count $k$ of class $j$ is the beta-binomial averaged over them,

$$
P^{(-j)}(k)=\sum_{ab}w^{(-j)}_{ab}\binom{n_j}{k}\frac{B(\nu_bm_a+k,\,\nu_b(1-m_a)+n_j-k)}{B(\nu_bm_a,\,\nu_b(1-m_a))},
$$

and the two-sided p-value of the observed count is $\min\{1,\,2\min(\sum_{k\le f_{jj}}P^{(-j)}(k),\sum_{k\ge f_{jj}}P^{(-j)}(k))\}$. A small p-value means that the other classes predict a recall for class $j$ that its own count contradicts. The harm of pooling such a class is largest in the bags dominated by it: at the vertex $\pi=e_j$ the accuracy of the bag is $r_j$, and UPP draws prevalences near the vertices often. The vertex shift $z_j$ measures the pull of the pooled prior on $r_j$ in units of its posterior standard deviation; when $\lvert z_j\rvert$ is above about $2$, the interval of a bag dominated by class $j$ is centred more than two of its own standard deviations from where $V$ alone would put it. With $K=2$ the test has a single other class to learn from and little power, which is itself a finding.

**D3: where the sampler would struggle.** Two features of the posterior make NUTS diverge. A Beta density with a parameter below $1$ grows without bound at the edge of $[0,1]$ (§T.11); with $m$ close to $1$ and $\nu$ moderate, $\nu(1-m)<1$, and the recall's density is singular at $1$. And when the posterior of $\nu$ spans several orders of magnitude, the recalls are tied to $m$ for large $\nu$ and free for small $\nu$, a narrow neck opening into a wide region (Neal's funnel, §T.12), in which no single step size works. The posterior probability $P(\min(\nu m,\nu(1-m))<1\mid V)=\sum_{ab}w_{ab}\,\mathbf 1[\min(\nu_bm_a,\nu_b(1-m_a))<1]$ measures the first, the width of the posterior of $\ln\nu$ the second. The stored empirical-Bayes runs are the plug-in version of the same prior: where their $(\hat m,\hat\nu)$ had a parameter below $1$, they diverged (E2.4, E2.5). If the hierarchical indicator is high on the same pairs, the same problem is expected; if averaging over $\nu$ puts little mass there, it is not.

- E2.4 adds the rows of `BayesCAP-EB` and `BayesCAP-EB-real` and reruns `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real` to store the NUTS diagnostics; before the stored results of `BayesCAP` are deleted, `scripts/bench/xla_vs_stored.py` must show that `BayCAP(estimand="population")` reproduces them, so that the rerun changes no stored measure and adds only the diagnostic columns; the paper's tables are then unaffected. The rerun of 2026-10-26 reproduces the scores used by E2.3 (identical coverage on every pair, Winkler scores within $2\cdot10^{-15}$). The diagnostics are computed outside the timed calls, so `t_test_ave` keeps its meaning; the E2.2 outputs of `BayesCAP` and `BayesCAP-real` are unchanged, since their prior part is the $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ one as before;
- every row records the prior, the seeds and the pushforward (population or predictive);
- E2.5 adds new pretrained classifiers and a new experiment folder and leaves the pretrained classifiers, results, tables and ranks of `main` unchanged (the protocol functions of `main` gained only an argument for the results folder, whose default keeps the paths of `main`); its bags follow the protocol of `main` on new datasets, so its pairs are comparable with the binary pairs of E2.4 but are not part of the grid of `main`.

### A.3 A finite test pool

`uv run python scripts/toys/ext2_test_pool.py` (defaults: $200$ replications of $20$ bags, $\rho=0.5$, seed $0$; run on 2026-10-28) isolates cause 4 (§T.13) with the uniform prior, on the same binary problem sized like `mammographic`. Each replication draws one $V$ and one test pool with $P_j$ instances per class, shared by $5$ classifiers with recalls between $0.76$ and $0.88$ and by $20$ bags drawn with replacement from the pool. Instances have a hidden difficulty shared by the classifiers: instance $i$ is classified correctly by classifier $k$ when $\sqrt\rho\,u_i+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,e_{ik}$ is below a threshold set by its recall, with $u_i$ and $e_{ik}$ independent standard normals, so the scores of two classifiers on the same instance have correlation $\rho$ and the classifiers tend to err on the same instances. The pool-aware interval is a reference: it replaces each draw of the recalls by a draw of the pool's recalls, $g_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(P_j,r_j)/P_j$, which needs $P_j$. "Low" is the share of (replication, classifier) pairs whose coverage over the bags is below $0.5$; "sd shift" is the standard deviation over pairs of the shift (posterior-mean accuracy minus realised accuracy, averaged over the bags), "predicted" its value from §T.13, $\sqrt{\sum_j\frac14\bar r_j(1-\bar r_j)(1/n^s_j+1/P_j)}$; "corr" and "slope" relate the shift to the gap $\overline{\sum_j\pi_j(\tilde r_j-g_j)}$; "corr. clf." is the mean correlation of the shifts of two classifiers across replications, "same sign" the share of replications in which all five shifts have the same sign ($2/32\approx0.06$ for independent classifiers).
