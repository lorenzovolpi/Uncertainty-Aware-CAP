# E3.c5: the full joint posterior of prevalence and weights

## What it is, in plain terms

E3.c4 imagines alternative validation sets by re-weighting the points of $V$. The weights come from $V$ alone, and the target bag $U$ never gets a say. E3.c5 lets $U$ have a say: the weights and the prevalence are estimated together, from $V$ and $U$ jointly.

**Intuition.** Under prior probability shift, the outputs of class $\omega_j$ have the same density in the source and in the target. So the target outputs are themselves extra evidence about that density. If many target outputs fall near a validation point $v$ of class $\omega_j$, that point was probably "under-represented" in $V$ by chance, and its weight should go up. BayesianCC does the same for $C$: its posterior on $C$ starts from the counts on $V$ and is then corrected by the predictions on $U$.

**The model.**

- **Unknowns:** the prevalence $\pi$ ($K-1$ free numbers) and, for each class, the weights $w_j=(w_{j,v})_{v\in V_j}$ ($n^s_j-1$ free numbers each), so $K-1+\lvert V\rvert-K$ in total.
- **Prior on $\pi$:** $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$, as in BayesKDEy.
- **Prior on the weights:** $w_j\sim\operatorname{Dir}(1,\dots,1)$, the Bayesian bootstrap of E3.c4. This is what $V$ alone says about the density of class $\omega_j$.
- **Likelihood of the target outputs:** the KDE mixture with weighted bumps,

$$\log\mathcal L_U(\pi,w)=\sum_{i=1}^{N^Q}\log\sum_j\pi_j\sum_{v\in V_j}w_{j,v}\,K_b(z_i,z_v).$$

- **Posterior:** $p(\pi,w\mid U,V)\propto p(\pi)\,p(w\mid V)\,\mathcal L_U(\pi,w)$. NUTS would sample $\pi$ and $w$ together.

The pushforward would use $C^{(w)}_{ij}=\sum_{v\in V_j}w_{j,v}\mathbf 1[h(v)=\omega_i]$ computed from the same draw. So $C$ and $\pi$ come out of one posterior, as in BayCAP.

## A reformulation that shows where the difficulty is

Look at the likelihood: it depends on $\pi$ and $w$ only through the products

$$\beta_v=\pi_{j(v)}\,w_{j(v),v},\qquad v\in V,$$

where $j(v)$ is the class of $v$. Because $\sum_v\beta_v=\sum_j\pi_j\sum_{v\in V_j}w_{j,v}=\sum_j\pi_j=1$, the target density is

$$g(z)=\sum_{v\in V}\beta_v\,K_b(z,z_v),$$

a single weighted KDE over all of $V$. The prevalence is then the total weight of each class: $\pi_j=\sum_{v\in V_j}\beta_v$.

So **E3.c5 models the target as a re-weighting of the validation points**, and $\pi$ is read off by summing the weights class by class. This explains both of its problems:

1. **Many weights the data cannot tell apart.** Take two validation points that are close in output space, say closer than the bandwidth $b$. Their bumps are almost identical, so the likelihood barely changes when weight moves from one to the other. The target bag pins down the weights only along directions where the bumps differ. Along all the other directions the posterior is flat, and only the prior holds it. NUTS struggles with this geometry: long flat directions next to tight ones force small steps and long trajectories. Funnels near the edges of the Dirichlet (weights close to 0) make it worse.
2. **The trade-off between $\pi$ and $w$.** Moving weight from a class-1 point to a nearby class-2 point leaves $g$ unchanged but changes $\pi$. Only the prior on $w$ resists this. That is legitimate: it is exactly the uncertainty about $\pi$ that E3.c5 wants to represent. But it makes the posterior hard to sample.

The cost per step is not the main issue. The kernel matrix $K_b(z_i,z_v)$, of size $N^Q\times\lvert V\rvert$, is computed once, and each gradient is a matrix-vector product. The cost comes from the number of steps NUTS needs to cross a badly shaped high-dimensional posterior.

## Ways to keep the dimension under control

All of these are new hypotheses.

### 1. Group the validation points into cells, and give weights to the cells

Merge the points of each class into $B_j$ groups (bins or clusters in output space). Keep the shape of each group fixed: within group $g$, the points keep equal weights. Give one weight to each group.

**Why this is exact for the prior.** The aggregation property of the Dirichlet says that summing the coordinates of a $\operatorname{Dir}(1,\dots,1)$ by groups gives a Dirichlet whose parameters are the group sizes. So the group weights have the prior

$$(\omega_{j1},\dots,\omega_{jB_j})\sim\operatorname{Dir}(m_{j1},\dots,m_{jB_j}),$$

where $m_{jg}$ is the number of points in group $g$. This is not a new assumption; it is what the Bayesian bootstrap already implies. What is lost is only the re-weighting inside each group.

**Why little is lost.** By the argument of point 1 above, the target cannot tell apart the weights of points that are much closer than $b$. Merging them removes exactly the directions the data cannot identify. The number of unknowns drops from about $\lvert V\rvert$ to $K-1+\sum_j(B_j-1)$. A grouping on the scale of $b$ keeps almost all of the information.

### 2. A special grouping: by the prediction of $h$

Choose as groups of class $\omega_j$ the instances that $h$ predicts as $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_K$. Then $B_j=K$, the group sizes are $f_{1j},\dots,f_{Kj}$, and the group weights are exactly the column $C_{\cdot j}$:

$$C_{\cdot j}\sim\operatorname{Dir}(f_{1j},\dots,f_{Kj}),\qquad f_j(z)=\sum_iC_{ij}\,\hat f_{ij}(z),$$

where $\hat f_{ij}$ is the KDE of the points of class $\omega_j$ that $h$ predicts as $\omega_i$.

This becomes a natural model if we also use the prediction $\hat y_i$ of $h$ on each target point. The density of the pair $(z_i,\hat y_i)$ is

$$p(z_i,\hat y_i=\omega_k)=\sum_j\pi_j\,C_{kj}\,\hat f_{kj}(z_i).$$

The derivation uses the law of total probability twice:

1. pick a true class $\omega_j$, with probability $\pi_j$;
2. given that class, $h$ predicts $\omega_k$ with probability $C_{kj}$;
3. given both, the output follows the density $\hat f_{kj}$.

Summing over the true classes gives the formula.

What this gives:

- **Size:** $K-1+K(K-1)$ unknowns, the same as BayesianCC, whatever $\lvert V\rvert$ is.
- **One coherent posterior:** $C$ and $\pi$ come out of the same posterior. The predictions of $h$ on $U$ correct $C$ as in BayesianCC, and the outputs on $U$ add the information of KDEy. This removes the independence problem of E3.c3 and the missing feedback from $U$ of E3.c4. It works both for $s=h$ and for $s$ an MLP.
- **Prior on $C$:** this is the place to choose $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f)$ or $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+1/K)$, the zero-cell question already open for E3.c3. With $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ it would bring back BayCAP's pull towards $1/K$.
- **What it does not represent:** the re-weighting inside each cell, i.e. the part of $e_1^V$ about the shape of $\hat f_{kj}$. Finer groups within each cell (point 1) would add it back at the cost of more unknowns.

I find this the most promising variant: it is BayCAP with a KDE likelihood, or BayesKDEy with a random $C$.

### 3. Keep all $\lvert V\rvert$ weights but use a Gibbs sampler instead of NUTS

Introduce, for each target point, the validation point whose bump "generated" it: a latent label $a_i\in V$. Given the labels, everything is conjugate:

- each label is drawn from $P(a_i=v\mid\beta)\propto\beta_v\,K_b(z_i,z_v)$;
- the weights within each class are updated as $w_j\mid a\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1+c_j)$, where $c_{j,v}$ counts the target points assigned to $v$;
- the prevalence is updated as $\pi\mid a\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1+(\sum_{v\in V_j}c_{j,v})_j)$.

Conjugate updates do not care about the dimension. Each sweep costs one pass over the kernel matrix, with no gradients and no step size to tune. The structure is the same as the EM of EMQ, but it samples instead of maximising. There are two caveats:

- **Mixing:** Gibbs moves slowly along the $\pi$-$w$ trade-off, the same flat directions that trouble NUTS. Its convergence must be checked with $\hat R$ and the effective sample size on $\pi$.
- **Custom sampler:** it is our own code, outside NumPyro and QuaPy, so it needs its own tests.

### 4. A parametric deformation of the densities

Replace the free weights with a few parameters, $w_{j,v}\propto\exp(\theta_j^\top\phi(z_v))$, where $\phi$ is, for example, the output itself. This is an exponential tilt of each class density, with about $K$ parameters per class. It is cheap, but it loses the clean link with the Bayesian bootstrap: the prior on $\theta$ must be chosen, with a scale of about $1/\sqrt{n^s_j}$ to mimic the error from $V$. And it represents only the deformations the tilt allows.

### 5. Variational inference

This is fast in any dimension, but standard mean-field approximations are known to underestimate posterior variance. Variance is exactly what we are trying to recover here, so I would not use it.

## My view

E3.c5 is worth pursuing only in the form of option 2, possibly refined with option 1. In that form:

- the full joint posterior is reduced to a BayesianCC-sized model with a KDE likelihood;
- E3.c3 and E3.c4 become special cases that switch off the feedback from $U$;
- the question "how much does it matter that $U$ updates $C$" can be asked directly.

Option 3 is the fallback if the full $\lvert V\rvert$ re-weighting turns out to matter, which a toy could test first.

A precise ordering is to be discussed. Option 2 might make E3.c3 and E3.c4 less interesting as end goals, and keep them only as ablations.

# The prior on $C$ in E3.c5, option 2

## What $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f)$ means

On the validation set, count the instances of true class $\omega_j$ that $h$ predicts as each class:

$$\mathbf f_{\cdot j}=(f_{1j},\dots,f_{Kj}),\qquad \sum_if_{ij}=n^s_j.$$

$\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f_{\cdot j})$, written $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f)$ for short, is a Dirichlet distribution for column $j$ of $C$ whose parameters are these counts. A draw from it is a column: non-negative numbers $C_{1j},\dots,C_{Kj}$ that sum to one.

## Where it comes from: the update from $V$

Start from a base prior $C_{\cdot j}\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)$, chosen before seeing any data. The validation counts are multinomial: each of the $n^s_j$ instances lands in row $i$ with probability $C_{ij}$, so

$$P(\mathbf f_{\cdot j}\mid C_{\cdot j})\propto\prod_iC_{ij}^{f_{ij}}.$$

The Dirichlet density is proportional to $\prod_iC_{ij}^{\alpha-1}$. Bayes' rule multiplies prior and likelihood, and the exponents add:

$$p(C_{\cdot j}\mid V)\propto\prod_iC_{ij}^{\alpha-1}\prod_iC_{ij}^{f_{ij}}=\prod_iC_{ij}^{f_{ij}+\alpha-1},$$

which is again a Dirichlet:

$$C_{\cdot j}\mid V\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f_{\cdot j}+\alpha).$$

So what I called "the prior on $C$" in option 2 is really **what $V$ says about $C$ before the target bag is seen**: the base prior updated with the validation counts. The target bag $U$ then updates it further through the likelihood of option 2, as in BayCAP. The choice is the value of $\alpha$:

- **$\alpha=1$:** BayCAP's flat base prior, giving $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+1)$.
- **$\alpha\to0$:** $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f)$. This is the Bayesian bootstrap of E3.c3 and E3.c4. Its base prior (Haldane's) is not a proper distribution, but the result is proper whenever every $f_{ij}>0$.
- **In between:** $\alpha=1/2$ (Jeffreys' prior) or $\alpha=1/K$ (one imaginary instance spread over the whole column).

## What $\alpha$ changes

Each coordinate of a Dirichlet is a Beta. The recall $C_{jj}$ has

$$\text{mean}=\frac{f_{jj}+\alpha}{n^s_j+K\alpha},\qquad \text{variance}=\frac{\mu(1-\mu)}{n^s_j+K\alpha+1}.$$

The mean can be rewritten as a weighted average of the observed recall $\hat d_j=f_{jj}/n^s_j$ and $1/K$:

$$\mu=(1-\lambda_j)\,\hat d_j+\lambda_j\,\frac1K,\qquad \lambda_j=\frac{K\alpha}{n^s_j+K\alpha}.$$

$\lambda_j$ is the **pull towards $1/K$**. It is large when $K\alpha$ is large compared with the class size. Here are the pull weights for the three cases of E3.1. This is plain arithmetic; I am assuming $K=10$ for `semeion` and `poker_hand`.

| case                         | $K$ | $n^s_j$ | $\alpha=1$ | $\alpha=1/2$ | $\alpha=1/K$ | $\alpha=0$ |
| ---------------------------- | --- | ------- | ---------- | ------------ | ------------ | ---------- |
| `mammographic`               | 2   | 145     | 0.014      | 0.007        | 0.007        | 0          |
| `semeion`, small class       | 10  | 56      | 0.152      | 0.082        | 0.018        | 0          |
| `poker_hand`, smallest class | 10  | 3       | 0.769      | 0.625        | 0.250        | 0          |

Take a recall of $0.9$ on the `semeion` class:

- **$\alpha=1$** moves its mean to $0.848\cdot0.9+0.152\cdot0.1\approx0.78$. That is the large-$K$ displacement of BayCAP found in E2.2.
- **$\alpha=1/K$** gives $0.886$.

The variance barely depends on $\alpha$ in any of these cases, so $\alpha$ mostly decides the bias, not the width.

## A reason to prefer $\alpha$ close to $0$: nesting with E3.1

In option 2 the density of class $\omega_j$ is $f_j(z)=\sum_iC_{ij}\hat f_{ij}(z)$. Set $C_{ij}=\hat C_{ij}=f_{ij}/n^s_j$, and write $\hat f_{ij}$ as the average of the bumps of its $f_{ij}$ points:

$$\sum_i\frac{f_{ij}}{n^s_j}\cdot\frac1{f_{ij}}\sum_{v\in V_{ij}}K_b(z,z_v)=\frac1{n^s_j}\sum_{v\in V_j}K_b(z,z_v),$$

which is exactly the KDE that BayesKDEy uses. So option 2 with $C$ held at its mean $\hat C$ is BayCAP-KDEy of E3.1. With $s=h$ this holds exactly, because the prediction of $h$ is a function of the output. With $s$ an MLP, option 2 also uses the predictions of $h$ on $U$.

With $\alpha=0$ the mean of $C$ is $\hat C$, so E3.1 sits at the centre of E3.c5, and the comparison isolates what the randomness of $C$ and the update from $U$ add. With $\alpha>0$ the centre shifts by the pull above. That shift is negligible except for tiny classes.

## The problem with $\alpha=0$: empty cells

If $f_{kj}=0$, then $C_{kj}$ is exactly $0$ in every draw: a Dirichlet coordinate with parameter $0$ is a point mass at $0$. In option 2 this has two consequences:

1. **The update from $U$ can never fill the cell.** The target bag can never "explain" a point predicted $\omega_k$ as coming from class $\omega_j$, however strongly its output suggests it. For a small class whose validation instances were all predicted correctly, the recall is $1$ in every draw, and E3.c5 would add no uncertainty where $V$ is weakest.
2. **The worst case: an undefined posterior.** If $h$ never predicts $\omega_k$ on $V$ (the whole row $k$ is empty) but predicts it on some target point, that point has likelihood $0$ under every $(\pi,C)$, and the posterior is undefined. This can happen with rare classes, for instance on `poker_hand`.

With $\alpha>0$ the empty cell gets a positive weight, but a second issue appears that is specific to option 2: **the cell has no points, so $\hat f_{kj}$ has no density to use.** It needs a convention. One natural choice [new hypothesis] is to borrow the KDE of all validation points that $h$ predicts as $\omega_k$, whatever their true class, written $\hat f_{k\cdot}$:

- **With $s=h$:** these outputs lie in the region where $h$ predicts $\omega_k$, as the outputs of the cell would.
- **With $s$ an MLP:** it is still the best available description of "outputs of instances predicted $\omega_k$".
- **Row $k$ empty as well:** the convention has nothing to borrow, and that case needs a separate guard. One option is the KDE of all of $V$; another is to exclude the method for that bag.

# Question 1
> here we could in some way learn the recalls and the shares of C_{ij, i\neq j} like we did with BayCAP-H(f), what do you think? is there a simpler way of incorporating the prior knowledge of C here given the model is different?

## Short answer

Yes, and it costs almost nothing extra. Option 2 has exactly the unknowns of BayCAP, $\pi$ and the columns of $C$, and it uses the validation counts the same way. The only thing it changes is how the target bag enters the likelihood. So any prior on $C$ that BayCAP has, including Hf, can be used unchanged. The prior also matters less here than in BayCAP, because the target bag says much more about $C$. That changes where a hierarchical prior helps: mostly in the small and empty cells.

## 1. Why Hf plugs in directly

Look at BayCAP's model in `src/uncawe/methods/baycap.py:529`. It has three parts:

1. **Prior.** $\pi\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$, and the columns of $C$ come from the prior chosen by `c_prior`: uniform, empirical Bayes, H or Hf (lines 540–545 and the hierarchical model at line 553).
2. **Validation counts as data.** $\mathbf f_{\cdot j}\sim\operatorname{Mult}(n^s_j,C_{\cdot j})$, observed (line 548).
3. **Target bag as data.** $n^t\sim\operatorname{Mult}(N^Q,C\pi)$, observed (line 551).

The $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+\alpha)$ of my last message is just parts 1 and 2 combined when the prior is $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$, by the conjugacy derived there. With Hf, part 1 is instead the hierarchy: recalls $r_j\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\nu m,\nu(1-m))$ and mistake shares $s_j\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\kappa\mathbf 1)$ with $C_{ij}=(1-r_j)s_{ij}$. Part 2 stays the same.

Option 2 replaces only part 3, with the KDE-cell likelihood

$$\log\mathcal L_U(\pi,C)=\sum_{u\in U}\log\sum_j\pi_j\,C_{\hat y_u j}\,\hat f_{\hat y_u j}(z_u).$$

In code this would be one alternative likelihood term in BayCAP's model (a `numpyro.factor` with the kernel values computed beforehand), and every `c_prior` would come for free. That is the simplest way, in implementation terms, to bring in prior knowledge on $C$. It also gives two clean one-factor comparisons:

- **Option 2 with Hf vs `BayesCAP-Hf-real`:** the same prior on $C$, the same use of $V$ and the same pushforward. Only the target-bag likelihood differs: the counts of predictions vs the KDE of the outputs within each cell.
- **Option 2 with $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+\alpha)$ vs BayCAP-KDEy realised (E3.1):** the same KDE. What differs is that $C$ is random and the target bag updates it.

## 2. Why the prior matters less here than in BayCAP

**In BayCAP**, the target bag enters only through $n^t$, the counts of predicted classes. That gives $K-1$ free numbers. Their expectation is $q=C\pi$. The unknowns $(\pi,C)$ have $K-1+K(K-1)$ free numbers, so the target bag fixes only $K-1$ combinations of them. Everything else comes from $V$ and the prior. With small classes on $V$ the prior decides a lot, which is why the uniform prior displaced BayCAP at large $K$ (E2.2) and why Hf was needed.

**In option 2, with $s=h$**, consider the target points that $h$ predicts as $\omega_k$. Their outputs follow the mixture

$$\sum_j\beta_{kj}\,\hat f_{kj}(z),\qquad\beta_{kj}=\pi_j\,C_{kj}.$$

If the shapes $\hat f_{k1},\dots,\hat f_{kK}$ are different from one another, the mixture identifies each weight $\beta_{kj}$. That is what a KDE quantifier does in each cell. This gives $K^2$ numbers that sum to one. From them:

- the prevalences are $\pi_j=\sum_k\beta_{kj}$, because each column of $C$ sums to one;
- the rates are $C_{kj}=\beta_{kj}/\pi_j$.

So the target bag can, in principle, identify both $\pi$ and $C$, instead of only $C\pi$, and the prior on $C$ becomes a regulariser rather than the main source of information.

**The caveat.** This works only as well as the shapes $\hat f_{kj}$ are distinct and well estimated. Two kinds of cell are weak:

- **Cells with many points but similar shapes.** For example, the outputs of $h$ on class $\omega_1$ and class $\omega_2$ that both fall in the region where $h$ predicts $\omega_1$ may look alike. The target bag then cannot split their weights, and $V$ and the prior take over again.
- **Cells with few or no points on $V$.** Their shape is badly known or borrowed, so here too the prior decides.

So the prior does most of its work exactly on the small and empty cells. That is also where Hf was designed to help.

## 3. What Hf would bring here, and a correction to my last message

**Small classes.** With $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+\alpha)$, a class with 3 instances, all correct, gets its recall pulled towards $1/K$. With Hf it is pulled towards $m$, the typical recall of the classifier, by an amount set by $\nu$. $\nu$ is learned from how similar the recalls of the other classes are. For a classifier with recalls around $0.6$ on `poker_hand`, pulling to $m$ is far more sensible than pulling to $1/K=0.1$.

**Empty off-diagonal cells.** With $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+\alpha)$ every empty cell of a column gets the same small mass. With Hf, $\kappa$ learns from $V$ whether the mistakes of this classifier are sparse or spread out.

**Correction.** I recommended $\alpha=1/K$ without flagging its sampler risk. An empty cell then has posterior parameter $f_{ij}+\alpha=1/K<1$. That is the long-tail problem of §T.11 in Ext. 2, which caused divergences with the empirical-Bayes prior. Hf has the same risk where $\kappa<1$ meets empty cells (§T.16). The non-centred shares of E2.9 address it, but they were only piloted on 4 pairs. In option 2 the target bag may pin some of those cells and soften the problem. That is a [new hypothesis], to check in a pilot.

This risk can't be avoided by choosing $\alpha$ alone. The total off-diagonal mass in a column has two effects: it pulls the recall towards $1/K$, and it puts mass in the empty cells. A single $\alpha$ must trade one against the other. Hf breaks the trade-off by learning $m$, $\nu$ and $\kappa$.

## 4. A simpler middle ground, if Hf is too heavy

Here is a fixed informative prior [new hypothesis]: centre the base Dirichlet on the pooled recall $\hat m$ of $V$, and give it a fixed total strength $a$, say $a=1$:

$$\alpha_{jj}=a\,\hat m,\qquad\alpha_{ij}=a\,\frac{1-\hat m}{K-1}\quad(i\ne j).$$

- **Pull.** The recall is pulled towards $\hat m$ instead of $1/K$, with weight $a/(n^s_j+a)$. For the 3-instance class that is $0.25$, towards a sensible value.
- **Compared with empirical Bayes.** It is the empirical-Bayes prior of E2.4 with the strength fixed at $a$ instead of fitted. So it cannot collapse at $K=2$, which is the failure of §T.10, where the fitted strength hit its bound.
- **What it keeps.** It still has parameters below 1 in the empty cells, it uses $V$ twice (mildly: one number, $\hat m$), and it does not learn sparsity.

It is a one-line change once the likelihood of option 2 sits inside BayCAP's model.

## My recommendation

1. **Build option 2 as an alternative target-bag likelihood inside BayCAP's model**, so that the prior is a switch.
2. **Run two priors first:**
   - **Hf**, compared with `BayesCAP-Hf-real`, to isolate the KDE likelihood against the best-covering reference;
   - **$\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf f+\alpha)$ with a small $\alpha$**, compared with E3.1, to isolate a random $C$ updated by the target bag.
3. **Measure, as a label-free diagnostic, how far the posterior of $C$ moves from what $V$ alone says**, cell by cell, against the number of validation points in the cell. That shows directly where the target bag identifies $C$ and where the prior decides.

Two things are needed whatever the prior:

- the empty-cell density convention (borrowing $\hat f_{k\cdot}$ or another choice);
- the guard for a predicted class that never occurs on $V$.
