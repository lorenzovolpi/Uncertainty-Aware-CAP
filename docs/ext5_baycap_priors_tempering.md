# Extension 5: Prevalence priors and tempering for BayCAP-Hf and BayCAP-KDEy

## Claim

The two Bayesian CAP methods kept by Extensions 2 and 3, BayCAP-Hf (`BayesCAP-Hf-real`, [[ext2_baycap|Extension 2]] E2.8) and BayCAP-KDEy with an MLP input and a fixed $\hat C$ (`BayCAP-KDEy-mlp-real`, [[ext3_posterior_propagation|Extension 3]] E3.1), both put a uniform prior on the target prevalence $\pi^Q$ and use their likelihoods at full strength ($\tau=1$). The paper names informative priors and posterior tempering as unexplored features of BayCAP. This extension measures two things, one experiment each: how much the knowledge of the distribution of the target prevalences improves the intervals of the two methods, when the prior on $\pi^Q$ is that distribution (E5.1); and how much tempering, which widens the posterior by giving the data less weight, improves them when the temperature is chosen on the labelled bags of the other datasets, without the labels of the target dataset (E5.2), as a default hyperparameter is tuned on a labelled benchmark. A third experiment (E5.3, the former candidate E5.c2, promoted by the user on 2026-11-15) chooses $\tau$ for each pair on labelled bags drawn from a held-out half of $V$, the only fully label-free choice, because E5.2 found that the best $\tau$ depends on the pair. Theory predicts small effects in both cases (§T.2, §T.6), except for the prior with many classes and small bags: a prior on $\pi^Q$ weighs little against a bag of $1000$ instances, and neither a prior on $\pi^Q$ nor a wider posterior moves an interval displaced by the error that comes from $V$, which is where both methods fail. A fourth experiment (E5.4, promoted directly by the user on 2026-11-18) asks about a different kind of knowledge of the prevalences, how extreme the shift is rather than its direction: the bags are drawn from a distribution concentrated near the centre of the simplex or from a sparse one near its faces, and each is analysed with a well-informed and an ill-informed prior. A fifth experiment (E5.5, decided by the user on 2026-11-19) asks whether a prior that knows only the side of the shift, less extreme than the truth, still helps: the bags come from $\operatorname{Dir}(10\cdot\mathbf 1)$ or $\operatorname{Dir}(\tfrac1{10}\mathbf 1)$, the priors stay those of E5.4, and the SQS methods are added, SQS-B with the priors and SQS-H, SQS-Hbs and SQS-S, which take no prior, as references. The extension is kept deliberately thin (user's decision of 2026-11-10): the prior on $C$ and the comparison of tempering with the realised-table predictive of the earlier version are set aside.

# Experiments

**Common settings.** The two methods as they are in `main`: `BayesCAP-Hf-real` (the Hf prior on $C$, centred shares, the NUTS settings of E2.8) and `BayCAP-KDEy-mlp-real` (BayesKDEy at the Gaussian kernel, $b=0.1$, $\lambda=0$, the MLP input trained on $V$, the fixed $\hat C$ of $h$ on $V$), both with the realised pushforward. The $75$ (dataset, classifier) pairs of `main` ($15$ datasets, five classifiers) and $100$ bags per pair and configuration. Measures for vanilla accuracy, macro-F1 and macro-K: the absolute error (AE) of the point estimate, coverage, amplitude and Winkler score at $\alpha=0.05$. Every score is computed against the realised table of each bag (`true_cts`), the quantity that both methods target. The five pairs of a dataset share its $V$ and its test pool, so for effects that depend on $V$ there are about $15$ independent units, not $75$.

## E5.1: Knowledge of the prior on the prevalence

**Why.** Under the protocol of `main` the target prevalences are drawn uniformly on the simplex, so the uniform prior of both methods is already the well-specified one (§T.3), and the question "how much would knowing the prior help" cannot be asked there. When the prevalences come from a non-uniform distribution $G$ and the prior is $G$, the posterior is calibrated on average over the bags and is narrower than with the uniform prior; the gain depends on the prior weight $c/(c+N_{\text{eff}})$, where $N_{\text{eff}}$ is the number of perfectly labelled instances that the bag is worth (§T.2). With the concentration $c=3K$ of E5.1, the prediction is a small gain at $\lvert U\rvert=1000$ except where $N_{\text{eff}}$ is small (classes poorly separated by $h$ or by the MLP) or $K$ is large ($c=78$ at $K=26$, so $w\ge0.07$), a larger one at $\lvert U\rvert=100$ ($w\ge0.057$ at $K=2$, $w\ge0.44$ at $K=26$), and none on the pairs where the interval is displaced by the error from $V$ (§T.4). A prior with the wrong mean measures the cost of believing in wrong knowledge. $G$ is built so that it carries the same amount of information for every number of classes $K$ and differs from the uniform distribution on every dataset (user's decision of 2026-11-10, §T.3).

- **Design.**
  - **Inputs:** the $75$ pairs of `main` (pre-trained classifiers, $V$ and test pool of each dataset); for each pair and each bag size, $100$ bags drawn from the test pool at prevalences drawn from $G=\operatorname{Dir}(\lambda K\mu^\star)$, with $\lambda=3$. The mean $\mu^\star$ and a second mean $\mu^\dagger$, used by the misspecified prior, are fixed points of the simplex, one pair of points per dataset (shared by its five classifiers): both are drawn from $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ with a seed fixed per dataset and recorded with the results, and drawn again until each is at $L_1$ distance at least $0.5$ from the centre $\mathbf 1/K$ and they are at $L_1$ distance at least $0.5$ from each other (§T.3). The requested prevalences and the bags are drawn with seeded generators, as in `binform`, so the bags are the same for every prior and method. A class with a small $\mu^\star_k$ has a parameter $\lambda K\mu^\star_k$ below $1$ and is often almost absent from the bags; this is part of what the well-specified prior knows.
  - **Varied:** the prior on $\pi^Q$: uniform, $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ (the default); well specified, $\operatorname{Dir}(\lambda K\mu^\star)=G$; misspecified, $\operatorname{Dir}(\lambda K\mu^\dagger)$, with the same concentration as $G$ and a wrong mean; the bag size $\lvert U\rvert\in\{100,1000\}$; the method (BayCAP-Hf, BayCAP-KDEy). Held fixed: $\tau=1$, the bags, everything else as in `main`.
  - **Measures:** the common measures per bag, and per pair their means over the $100$ bags; per pair, the value of the knowledge, $\Delta W_{\text{know}}=W_{\text{uniform}}-W_{\text{well-specified}}$, the cost of wrong knowledge, $\Delta W_{\text{wrong}}=W_{\text{misspecified}}-W_{\text{uniform}}$ (both positive when they mean what their name says), and the ratio of the mean amplitude with the well-specified prior to that with the uniform prior; a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the $75$ per-pair means for each of the two differences, per method and bag size.
  - **Estimand:** the realised table of each bag, for both methods and every prior, scored against it.
  - **Cost:** $3$ priors $\times$ $2$ bag sizes $\times$ $2$ methods on the $75$ pairs, $900$ runs of $100$ bags; the uniform prior at $\lvert U\rvert=1000$ cannot reuse the results of `main`, whose bags are drawn uniformly. BayCAP-KDEy samples $\pi$ alone and is cheap; BayCAP-Hf costs, per prior and bag size, about one run of `BayesCAP-Hf-real` in `main`. A pilot on three datasets with the five classifiers first.

  What this shows: knowing the distribution of the target prevalences helps both methods on average, by recentring their intervals rather than narrowing them, but the gain is concentrated on a few datasets, mostly those where the uniform prior undercovers because of an error in the prevalence (`connect-4`, `poker_hand`, `wine-quality`); on a typical dataset it is close to zero and not significant over the $15$ datasets. Believing in a wrong distribution costs several times more than knowing the right one gains, on the same datasets, so an informative prior on $\pi^Q$ is worth using only when the distribution of the target prevalences is known with confidence. Two predictions of the theory fail: the gain does not grow with $K$, and it is largest on the pairs that undercover with the uniform prior, where the error is in the prevalence, rather than on the pairs that already cover; where the error is in the recalls (`mammographic`), the prior does not help, as §T.4 expects.

## E5.2: Tempering

**Why.** Tempering divides the log-likelihood by $\tau$, which is the same as pretending that the data are $\tau$ times smaller (§T.5): the centre of the posterior stays in place and its spread grows by about $\sqrt\tau$. BayCAP-KDEy has a single likelihood term, that of the bag, so tempering widens only the part of its interval that comes from $\pi$, and leaves out the error of the rates of $h$ on $V$ where E3.1 found it failing; where the luck of the bag dominates the interval, as on binary datasets, the interval barely widens (§T.6). BayCAP-Hf is tempered jointly, its validation term and its bag term both (user's decision of 2026-11-10), so the rates of $h$ become more uncertain too, which reaches the error from $V$ (§T.7). Tempering cannot move a displaced centre, so the question is whether the extra width buys enough coverage to lower the Winkler score, and with which $\tau$. A $\tau$ chosen with the target labels always looks at least as good as $\tau=1$, even when tempering does nothing (§T.8), so the tempered method is the one whose $\tau$ is chosen on the other datasets (leave one dataset out), and the $\tau$ chosen with the target labels is only a reference (user's decision of 2026-11-10). The selection uses the labels of the bags of the other datasets, so the tempered method assumes what a default tuned on a labelled benchmark assumes: that the best $\tau$ carries over to a new dataset, and that the shift met in use resembles the shift of the benchmark (§T.8); E5.2 tests the first assumption, not the second.

Here it would have been safe in one respect, since the same $\tau$ is chosen whatever dataset is held out; the second assumption, that the shift met in use resembles the benchmark's, is not tested by this experiment, since all datasets share the protocol of `main`.

A single $\tau$ for all datasets cannot do one without the other, and the datasets where it helps are few. The condition under which the selection on validation bags (then the candidate E5.c2) was to become worth its cost (tempering retained, with a $\tau_{-d}$ that varies across datasets) is not met; the user promoted it anyway on 2026-11-15, as E5.3, because the oracle-best $\tau$ varies across pairs.

## E5.3: Choosing the temperature per pair on validation bags

**Why.** E5.2 found that no single $\tau$ suits every dataset. The leave-one-dataset-out $\tau$ ($2$ for BayCAP-Hf, $8$ for BayCAP-KDEy) lowers the Winkler score on the few datasets whose interval is displaced and raises it slightly on the others (median per-dataset change $-0.002$ and $-0.003$), and the oracle-best $\tau$ of BayCAP-Hf is $0.5$ on $30$ pairs and between $2$ and $8$ on $21$. A $\tau$ chosen for each pair could keep the gains where the interval is displaced without the losses where it already covers. The only source for such a choice that uses no label outside $V$ is a set of labelled bags drawn from $V$; it is also the only fully label-free selection, since the leave-one-dataset-out $\tau$ uses the labels of the other datasets (§T.8). Bags drawn from the $V$ on which the method is fitted have no error from $V$ (§T.8), so $V$ is split into a half that fits the method and a half from which the calibration bags are drawn. The calibration chooses $\tau$ only: the deployed method is fitted on the full $V$, as a practitioner would do, and is the one of E5.2, whose stored results on the test bags are reused (user's decision of 2026-11-15). With this choice the error on the calibration bags is uncorrelated with the error of the deployed method on the test bags (§T.9): the calibration cannot tell whether the particular $V$ of a pair gave recalls that are too high or too low. What it sees is the typical size of the error from $V$ for that pair (large when the classes of $V$ are small, when $C$ is ill-conditioned, or when the recalls are far from $0$ and $1$) and any systematic bias of the method that the split does not change, such as a bias of the quantifier. So E5.3 tests a rule that sets the width of each pair's interval from the expected size of its error, not from its actual displacement (new hypothesis: the displaced datasets of E5.2 are those with a large expected error or a systematic bias). The calibration error has twice the standard deviation of the deployed error (§T.9), so the selection is expected to choose a $\tau$ larger than needed. Promoted from the candidate E5.c2 by the user on 2026-11-15, although the condition stated in E5.2 (tempering retained) is not met.

## E5.4: Concentrated and sparse prevalence distributions

**Why.** In E5.1 the right and the wrong prior have the same concentration and different means: they know *which* classes are large. E5.4 asks about another kind of knowledge, *how extreme* the shift is, which a user may have without knowing its direction: whether the target prevalences stay close to balance (mild shift) or are sparse, with a few classes dominating and the others nearly absent (severe shift). Two symmetric distributions, both with mean $\mathbf 1/K$, describe the two situations: a concentrated one, $G_c=\operatorname{Dir}(\lambda\mathbf 1)$ with $\lambda=3$, and a sparse one, $G_e=\operatorname{Dir}(\beta\mathbf 1)$ with $\beta=1/3$ (user's decision of 2026-11-18). $G_c$ is the $G$ of E5.1 with its mean moved to the centre ($3K\mu^\star$ with $\mu^\star=\mathbf 1/K$); $G_e$ puts its mass near the faces of the simplex. The bags are drawn from each, and each is analysed with the uniform prior, the well-informed prior (the distribution that generated the bags) and the ill-informed prior (the other one). The theory (§T.10) predicts that the four cells are not symmetric: $G_c$ adds $3K$ imaginary instances to the bag and pulls every prevalence towards $1/K$, while $G_e$ adds $K/3$, fewer than the uniform prior's $K$, and acts almost only on classes absent from the bag. So the expected outcome is a gain of the well-informed prior under $G_c$, smaller than in E5.1 if the uniform prior already does well near the centre; a small cost of the ill-informed prior under $G_c$ and a small gain of the well-informed prior under $G_e$, both visible only where $N_{\text{eff}}$ is small; and a large cost of the ill-informed prior under $G_e$, where the pull towards $1/K$ lifts the absent classes. As in E5.1, no gain is expected where the interval is displaced by the error from $V$ (§T.4).

## E5.5: Right-side and wrong-side priors under strong shifts, with SQS references

**Why.** In E5.4 the well-informed prior was the distribution of the bags itself, which a user rarely knows exactly. A user is more likely to know only on which side the shift lies, mild or severe, and to underestimate how far it goes. E5.5 asks whether a prior on the right side but less extreme than the truth still helps (user's decision of 2026-11-19). The bags are drawn from distributions more extreme than those of E5.4: a concentrated one, $G_c=\operatorname{Dir}(10\cdot\mathbf 1)$, and a sparse one, $G_e=\operatorname{Dir}(\tfrac1{10}\mathbf 1)$. The priors stay those of E5.4, $\operatorname{Dir}(3\cdot\mathbf 1)$ and $\operatorname{Dir}(\tfrac13\mathbf 1)$. On each set of bags, the prior on the same side as the distribution is the *right-side* prior and the other is the *wrong-side* prior; neither matches the distribution, and a matched prior is left out (user's decision of 2026-11-19). §T.11 predicts that a right-side prior is always better than the uniform one on average, and a wrong-side prior always worse, because the error of the posterior mean grows with the distance of the prior weight from that of the matched prior; the cost of the wrong-side prior is largest on sparse bags with many classes. To show that only methods that use a prior can gain from the information, E5.5 adds the SQS methods of `main`: SQS-B, which takes a prior on $\pi^Q$ through its Bayesian quantifier (BayesKDEy, as in BayCAP-KDEy), is run with the three priors; SQS-H, SQS-Hbs and SQS-S take no prior on $\pi^Q$ and give the same intervals whatever the information, so they are references against which the informed methods move. Only $\lvert U\rvert=1000$ is run, the bag size of `main` and of the validation bags of the SQS methods (user's decision of 2026-11-19); there the prior weighs least (§T.2), so the effects of the information are expected to be smaller than at $\lvert U\rvert=100$, except where $N_{\text{eff}}$ is small.

# Candidate experiments

Ideas that came up in the discussions; they are not scheduled. One becomes an experiment of the main section only when the user decides so.

- **E5.c1, tempering one likelihood term of BayCAP-Hf.** *Why:* joint tempering widens both the rates of $h$ and the prevalence; tempering $\mathcal L_U$ only leaves the validation counts at full weight, and $\mathcal L_V$ only widens the rates alone, so the two variants tell which term does the work, if joint tempering helps in E5.2. *Needs:* a flag of the `temperature` argument of `BayCAP` selecting the term, and two more sweeps of BayCAP-Hf on the protocol of E5.2.

# Theory

Notation of the reference paper: $C_{ij}=P(\hat Y=\omega_i\mid Y=\omega_j)$ for the classifier $h$, $d_j=C_{jj}$ its recall on class $\omega_j$, $\pi^Q$ the prevalence of the target bag $U$ of $N=\lvert U\rvert$ instances, and $T=C\operatorname{diag}(\pi^Q)$ the population table, with predicted classes on rows and true classes on columns. $\tau$ is the temperature.

## T.1 Where the prior and the temperature enter the two methods

**BayCAP-Hf.** The posterior is over $(\pi^Q,C)$ and the parameters $(m,\rho,\kappa)$ of the Hf prior on $C$ ([[ext2_baycap|Extension 2]] §T.16):

$$
p(\pi^Q,C,m,\rho,\kappa\mid V,U)\;\propto\;\underbrace{\mathcal L_V(C)}_{\text{counts of }V}\;\underbrace{\mathcal L_U(\pi^Q,C)}_{\text{predicted counts of }U}\;p(\pi^Q)\;p(C\mid m,\rho,\kappa)\;p(m,\rho,\kappa).
$$

$\mathcal L_V(C)$ is a product of multinomials: in each true class $\omega_j$ of $V$, the $n^s_j$ instances fall in the predicted classes with probabilities $C_{:j}$. $\mathcal L_U(\pi^Q,C)$ is the multinomial of the $N$ predictions of $h$ on $U$, with probabilities $C\pi^Q$. Joint tempering replaces the two likelihoods with $[\mathcal L_V\mathcal L_U]^{1/\tau}$ and leaves the priors unchanged.

**BayCAP-KDEy.** The rate matrix is held at $\hat C$, the rates of $h$ on $V$, and NUTS samples $\pi^Q$ alone, from

$$
p(\pi^Q\mid U)\;\propto\;\Big[\prod_{n=1}^N\sum_j\pi^Q_j\,\hat p_j(x_n)\Big]^{1/\tau}p(\pi^Q),
$$

where $x_n$ are the outputs of the MLP on the instances of $U$ and $\hat p_j$ the kernel density of those outputs within class $\omega_j$, estimated on $V$ ([[ext3_posterior_propagation|Extension 3]] §T.3). There is no validation term to temper: $V$ enters only through $\hat C$ and $\hat p_j$, which are fixed.

**The interval.** Both methods turn each posterior draw into a realised table of $U$ (the realised pushforward, [[ext2_baycap|Extension 2]] §T.3), and the interval is the percentile interval of the metric over these tables. In both methods the prior on $\pi^Q$ is $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)$, the argument `prior`, uniform ($\alpha=\mathbf 1$) by default. We write $\alpha=c\,\mu$, with $\mu$ the prior mean and $c=\sum_k\alpha_k$ its concentration; the uniform prior has $\mu=\mathbf 1/K$ and $c=K$.

## T.2 How much a prior on the prevalence weighs

**The simplest case.** Take a perfect and known classifier, so that the predicted counts $n_1,\dots,n_K$ of $U$ are its true class counts, multinomial with probabilities $\pi^Q$. The tempered likelihood is $\prod_k(\pi^Q_k)^{n_k/\tau}$ (T.5) and the prior density is proportional to $\prod_k(\pi^Q_k)^{c\mu_k-1}$. Multiplying the two, the posterior density is proportional to $\prod_k(\pi^Q_k)^{c\mu_k+n_k/\tau-1}$, which is again a Dirichlet (the Dirichlet is conjugate to the multinomial):

$$
\pi^Q\mid U\;\sim\;\operatorname{Dir}\!\Big(c\,\mu+\frac{n}{\tau}\Big).
$$

The mean of component $k$ of a $\operatorname{Dir}(a)$ is $a_k/\sum_la_l$. Here $\sum_la_l=c+N/\tau$, so

$$
\mathbb E[\pi^Q_k\mid U]=\frac{c\mu_k+n_k/\tau}{c+N/\tau}=w\,\mu_k+(1-w)\,\frac{n_k}{N},\qquad w=\frac{c}{c+N/\tau},
$$

where the second form follows by writing $c\mu_k=w(c+N/\tau)\mu_k$ and $n_k/\tau=(1-w)(c+N/\tau)\,n_k/N$. The **prior weight** $w$ is the share of the posterior mean that comes from the prior; the rest comes from the observed proportions. The variance of component $k$ of a $\operatorname{Dir}(a)$ is $\bar a_k(1-\bar a_k)/(\sum_la_l+1)$, with $\bar a_k$ its mean, so the posterior standard deviation is about

$$
\operatorname{sd}(\pi^Q_k\mid U)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\bar\pi_k(1-\bar\pi_k)}{c+N/\tau+1}} .
$$

*Intuition:* the prior acts as $c$ imaginary instances added to the $N/\tau$ real ones. With $c=10$ and $\tau=1$, $w=0.010$ at $N=1000$ and $w=0.091$ at $N=100$; at the same posterior mean, adding the prior shrinks the standard deviation by the factor $\sqrt{(N+1)/(N+c+1)}$, which is $0.995$ at $N=1000$ and $0.954$ at $N=100$.

**A real classifier is worth fewer instances.** With confusions, the predicted counts tell less about $\pi^Q$ than true labels would. In the binary case with recalls $d_1$ (class $\omega_1$) and $d_0$ (class $\omega_0$) and known, the probability that an instance is predicted as $\omega_1$ is

$$
q=d_1\pi_1+(1-d_0)(1-\pi_1)=(1-d_0)+\pi_1\,(d_1+d_0-1),
$$

so $\pi_1=(q-(1-d_0))/(d_1+d_0-1)$. The observed share $\hat q$ of predictions $\omega_1$ is a proportion of $N$ independent instances, with variance $q(1-q)/N$; the estimate $\hat\pi_1=(\hat q-(1-d_0))/(d_1+d_0-1)$ is a linear function of it, and $\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\pi_1)=\frac{q(1-q)}{N\,(d_1+d_0-1)^2}.
$$

With true labels it would be $\pi_1(1-\pi_1)/N$. Equating the two defines the **effective size** of the bag, the number of true labels that it is worth:

$$
N_{\text{eff}}=N\,(d_1+d_0-1)^2\,\frac{\pi_1(1-\pi_1)}{q(1-q)} .
$$

For example, $d_1=d_0=0.8$ and $\pi_1=0.5$ give $q=0.5$ and $N_{\text{eff}}=0.36\,N$: a bag of $100$ is worth $36$ labels, and the prior weight of $c=10$ becomes $10/(10+36)=0.22$. With $K$ classes the same holds direction by direction in the simplex, and the directions that $C$ separates worst (small singular values) have the smallest effective size. In BayCAP-KDEy the outputs of the MLP replace the predictions, and $N_{\text{eff}}$ is larger the less the densities $\hat p_j$ overlap; in BayCAP-Hf the uncertainty on $C$ lowers it further. The prior on $\pi^Q$ matters only where $N_{\text{eff}}/\tau$ is not much larger than $c$.

## T.3 Well-specified prior and calibration on average

*Intuition:* a prior is "correct" when it describes how often each target situation actually occurs; then, and only then, Bayesian intervals are calibrated on average over those situations.

In every experiment, bag prevalences are produced by a known mechanism: $\pi^Q_i\sim G$, then a bag $U_i$ with that prevalence is sampled. A prior is **well specified** when $p(\pi^Q)=G$. Under the protocol of `main`, $G=\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$, which is exactly the uniform prior of both methods: the paper already evaluates BayCAP with a well-specified prior on $\pi^Q$, and under that protocol any informative prior is misspecified by construction. This is why E5.1 draws its bags from a non-uniform $G$.

If the prior equals $G$ and the likelihood is the true sampling model, a $95\%$ credible interval contains the true value for exactly $95\%$ of the bags. The reason is Bayes' theorem: the posterior is then the actual conditional distribution of the true $\pi^Q$ among all bags that produce the same observed data. The guarantee is an average over bags drawn from $G$, not a property of each bag. A vague wrong prior usually keeps coverage and loses sharpness; a confident wrong prior can lose coverage.

Toy example (`scripts/toys/ext5_prior_calibration.py`): binary, perfect known classifier, $n\sim\operatorname{Bin}(20,\pi_1)$, Beta priors, $200{,}000$ bags, $95\%$ equal-tailed intervals.

| $G$ | prior | coverage | mean width | coverage for $\pi_1\in[0.4,0.6)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| uniform | uniform (well specified) | $0.950$ | $0.33$ | $0.95$ |
| uniform | $\operatorname{Beta}(2,8)$ | $0.65$ | $0.31$ | $0.83$ |
| $\operatorname{Beta}(2,8)$ | $\operatorname{Beta}(2,8)$ (well specified) | $0.950$ | $0.26$ | $0.86$ |
| $\operatorname{Beta}(2,8)$ | uniform | $0.955$ | $0.31$ | $0.95$ |
| $\operatorname{Beta}(2,8)$ | $\operatorname{Beta}(8,2)$ | $0.26$ | $0.34$ | $0.81$ |

With a non-uniform $G$ the uniform prior keeps coverage but its intervals are about $20\%$ wider than those of the well-specified prior: that difference is the value of the prior information, and the formula of T.2 predicts it ($n=20$, $c=10$: $\sqrt{21/31}=0.82$). With bags of $100$ or $1000$ instances the same formula predicts a much smaller value, unless $N_{\text{eff}}$ is small.

The guarantee has three conditions, and each can fail in the two methods: the prior must equal $G$; the likelihood must be the true sampling model (the rates and densities are estimated on $V$ and then treated as exact); and the interval must target the quantity that is scored (it does, with the realised pushforward). A well-specified prior removes only the first cause of miscalibration.

**The choice of $G$ in E5.1.** The amount of information that $G=\operatorname{Dir}(c\,\mu)$ adds to the uniform prior depends on its concentration $c$ compared with $K$, the concentration of the uniform prior. The variance of component $k$ of a $\operatorname{Dir}(c\,\mu)$ is $\mu_k(1-\mu_k)/(c+1)$ (T.2). At the same mean, the variance of the uniform prior divided by that of $G$ is therefore

$$
\frac{\mu_k(1-\mu_k)/(K+1)}{\mu_k(1-\mu_k)/(c+1)}=\frac{c+1}{K+1}.
$$

With a fixed concentration, such as the $c=10$ of `binform`, this ratio is $11/3\approx3.7$ at $K=2$, $1$ at $K=10$ (no information about the spread) and $11/27\approx0.4$ at $K=26$ ($G$ more spread than the uniform distribution, its parameters $10/26\approx0.38$ below $1$, so its bags lie near the faces of the simplex). The knowledge would then mean different things on different datasets, and nothing at $K=10$. With $c=\lambda K$ the ratio is $(\lambda K+1)/(K+1)$, which is $2.3$ at $K=2$ and tends to $\lambda$ as $K$ grows: with $\lambda=3$, $G$ is about three times less spread than the uniform prior on every dataset, and at $K=3$ its concentration, $9$, is close to that of `binform`. The mean of `binform`, $\mu^\star_k=(1-\pi^V_k)/(K-1)$, is close to the centre $\mathbf 1/K$ when $V$ is nearly balanced, since then $\pi^V_k\approx1/K$ and $(1-1/K)/(K-1)=1/K$; and its "no shift" prior, centred on $\pi^V$, is then close to $G$ itself. E5.1 therefore draws its two means as fixed points of the simplex, required to be at $L_1$ distance at least $0.5$ from the centre and from each other, so that the well-specified prior differs from the uniform one and the misspecified prior from the well-specified one on every dataset. In the binary case these conditions read $\lvert\mu^\star_1-\tfrac12\rvert\ge\tfrac14$, $\lvert\mu^\dagger_1-\tfrac12\rvert\ge\tfrac14$ and $\lvert\mu^\star_1-\mu^\dagger_1\rvert\ge\tfrac14$, since the $L_1$ distance between two binary prevalence vectors is twice the difference of their first components.

## T.4 What a prior on the prevalence cannot do

Take BayCAP-KDEy, where $C$ is fixed at $\hat C$ with recalls $\hat d_j$. The point estimate of accuracy is close to $\sum_j\hat\pi_j\hat d_j$, and the realised accuracy of $U$ is $A=\sum_j\pi^Q_j\,c_j/n_j$, with $n_j$ the instances of class $\omega_j$ in $U$ and $c_j$ those that $h$ classifies correctly. Adding and subtracting $\sum_j\pi^Q_j\hat d_j$ and $\sum_j\pi^Q_jd_j$, with $d_j$ the recalls on the test distribution,

$$
\sum_j\hat\pi_j\hat d_j-A=\underbrace{\sum_j(\hat\pi_j-\pi^Q_j)\,\hat d_j}_{\text{prevalence term}}+\underbrace{\sum_j\pi^Q_j(\hat d_j-d_j)}_{\text{recall term}}+\underbrace{\sum_j\pi^Q_j\Big(d_j-\frac{c_j}{n_j}\Big)}_{\text{luck of the bag}},
$$

the error split of [[ext4_uncertainty_budget|Extension 4]] (its parts $e_1$, $e_2$, $e_3$). The prior on $\pi^Q$ changes only $\hat\pi$, so it acts only on the prevalence term. On the pairs where E3.1 found BayCAP-KDEy failing (`mammographic`, `semeion` with LR and MLP), the error is in the recall term, which is the same for every prior; on `wine-quality` it is in the prevalence term, but as a bias that every quantifier shares, and a prior centred on $G$ does not know the bias of the quantifier. BayCAP-Hf represents the recall term through its posterior on $C$, but the prior on $\pi^Q$ still changes only the prevalence part. So E5.1 is expected to show its gains, if any, on pairs that already cover.

## T.5 Tempering: the data counted $\tau$ times less

For a multinomial with counts $n_1,\dots,n_K$ and probabilities $p_1,\dots,p_K$, the likelihood is, apart from a constant coefficient, $\prod_ip_i^{n_i}$. Raising it to the power $1/\tau$,

$$
\Big(\prod_ip_i^{n_i}\Big)^{1/\tau}=\prod_ip_i^{n_i/\tau},
$$

the likelihood of the same proportions observed on $N/\tau$ instances. The kernel likelihood of BayCAP-KDEy is a product over the instances of the bag, so raising it to $1/\tau$ also counts each instance as $1/\tau$ of an instance.

**One recall, step by step.** With a uniform prior on a recall $d$ and $f$ correct predictions out of $n$ instances, the tempered posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(f/\tau+1,(n-f)/\tau+1)$. Its mean is $(f/\tau+1)/(n/\tau+2)$, close to $f/n$ when $n/\tau$ is large: the centre stays in place. Its variance is $\bar d(1-\bar d)/(n/\tau+3)$, by the variance of the Beta distribution, so for $n/\tau$ large

$$
\operatorname{sd}(d\mid\text{data})\approx\sqrt{\frac{\tau\,\bar d(1-\bar d)}{n}}=\sqrt\tau\times\operatorname{sd}\text{ at }\tau=1 .
$$

**In general.** With many data the log-posterior is close to a quadratic function around its maximum (the normal approximation of the posterior), whose curvature is the Fisher information of the data, $I$; the posterior covariance is $I^{-1}$. Dividing the log-likelihood by $\tau$ divides the curvature by $\tau$, so the covariance becomes $\tau I^{-1}$ and every standard deviation grows by $\sqrt\tau$, while the maximum does not move. A value $\tau<1$ sharpens the posterior in the same way.

**The prior gains weight.** The prior is not tempered, so its imaginary instances are compared with $N/\tau$ real ones: the prior weight of T.2 grows from $c/(c+N)$ to $c/(c+N/\tau)$. With the uniform prior on $\pi^Q$ this pulls the posterior slightly towards the centre of the simplex; it is the reason for the candidate E5.c3.

## T.6 Tempering BayCAP-KDEy: how much the realised interval widens

**Two sources of spread.** Let $A$ be the realised accuracy drawn by the realised pushforward. Given a draw of $\pi$, the realised table is drawn around the population table, so $A$ has a conditional mean $\mu(\pi)=\sum_j\pi_j\hat d_j$ and a conditional variance $\sigma_g^2(\pi)$, the luck of the bag ([[ext3_posterior_propagation|Extension 3]] §T.8: it depends on $\pi$ only through the precisions of $h$, and is stored per bag as $\bar\sigma_g$). The law of total variance, $\operatorname{Var}(A)=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(A\mid\pi)]+\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[A\mid\pi])$, gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(A)=\bar\sigma_g^2+\sigma_p^2,
$$

where $\sigma_p^2$ is the posterior variance of $\mu(\pi)$, the spread of the population interval.

**Effect of $\tau$.** By T.5, tempering multiplies $\sigma_p^2$ by about $\tau$; $\bar\sigma_g^2$ depends on the observed predictions of $U$ and only weakly on $\pi$, so it stays about the same. The width of the interval is proportional to the standard deviation, so the widening with respect to $\tau=1$ is

$$
\frac{\text{width at }\tau}{\text{width at }1}\approx\sqrt{\frac{\tau\sigma_p^2+\bar\sigma_g^2}{\sigma_p^2+\bar\sigma_g^2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\tau+r^2}{1+r^2}},\qquad r=\frac{\bar\sigma_g}{\sigma_p},
$$

dividing numerator and denominator by $\sigma_p^2$. It lies between $1$ (the bag dominates, $r$ large) and $\sqrt\tau$ (the prevalence dominates, $r$ small).

**What the stored results predict.** E3.1 measured the widening of the realised over the population interval, $F=\sqrt{1+r^2}$, so $r^2=F^2-1$. With its medians for BayCAP-KDEy, $F=4.6$–$5.7$ at $K=2$ and $F=1.4$–$1.6$ at $3\le K\le7$ ([[ext3_posterior_propagation|Extension 3]], Analysis of E3.1, medians over the four realised methods), $\tau=4$ widens the interval by a factor $1.04$–$1.07$ at $K=2$ and $1.47$–$1.59$ at $3\le K\le7$ (derived from the formula, not measured). On binary datasets, where E3.1 found the failures from $V$, tempering BayCAP-KDEy is nearly inert. As a rough illustration, an interval displaced by $4.7$ of its standard deviations, as on `mammographic` in E3.1, needs about $2.4$ times its width to reach the target ($4.7/1.96$), which with the median $r^2$ of $K=2$ ($F=4.6$, $r^2=20.2$) requires $(\tau+20.2)/21.2\ge2.4^2$, i.e. $\tau\gtrsim100$.

## T.7 Tempering BayCAP-Hf jointly

Joint tempering divides the counts of $V$ and of $U$ by $\tau$ (T.5). The validation counts are what determine the rates of $h$, so the posterior standard deviation of each recall grows by about $\sqrt\tau$ where the data dominate the prior, and with it the part of the interval that represents the recall term of T.4, the error from $V$. This is the part that the fixed-$\hat C$ methods lack and that lets BayCAP-Hf cover more than BayCAP-KDEy (mean coverage $0.89$ against $0.74$ for vanilla accuracy in E3.1, with mean amplitude $0.126$ against $0.053$). The bag term $\bar\sigma_g^2$ is unchanged, as in T.6. Two side effects follow from the untempered prior: the hierarchical prior on the recalls gains weight with respect to the $n^s_j/\tau$ instances of each class, so the recalls are pooled more towards their common centre $m$; and the uniform prior on $\pi^Q$ gains weight as in T.5. Tempering moves no centre, so on pairs where the interval of BayCAP-Hf is displaced it helps only by widening; on pairs that over-cover, $\tau<1$ may narrow the interval without losing coverage.

## T.8 Choosing the temperature without the target labels

**Why the best $\tau$ in hindsight is optimistic.** For a pair $p$, the mean Winkler score $W_p(\tau)$ over its $100$ bags is the true score of the method plus a random error, because the bags are a random sample. Suppose tempering did nothing, so the five values $W_p(0.5),\dots,W_p(8)$ had the same true score and differed only by their errors. Their smallest value would still be below $W_p(1)$ in most pairs, since the minimum of five numbers is at most each of them, and below it with probability $4/5$ when the five errors are exchangeable. So the oracle-best $\tau$, chosen by looking at the scores of the pair, shows a gain even with no real effect. It is an upper bound, not evidence.

**Leave one dataset out.** For each dataset $d$ of the $15$:

1. take the results of the sweep on the other $14$ datasets ($70$ pairs);
2. for each $\tau$, average the per-pair mean Winkler scores over these $70$ pairs, and call $\tau_{-d}$ the value with the lowest average;
3. read the results of the five pairs of $d$ at $\tau_{-d}$.

No label of $d$ is used to choose $\tau_{-d}$, so on $d$ the selected method is evaluated as a practitioner would use it: with a temperature tuned on other problems, as a default hyperparameter is tuned on a benchmark.

**What the selection assumes.** The selection is not label-free: step 2 scores each $\tau$ by the Winkler score against the realised tables of the bags of the other datasets, which are computed from their labels. The method that is evaluated on $d$ is therefore BayCAP with a temperature fixed once by whoever designs the method, on a collection of labelled datasets with simulated shift, and then used unchanged. This is legitimate under two assumptions. (1) The best $\tau$ carries over from the benchmark datasets to a new dataset; leaving one dataset out tests exactly this, since $d$ plays the new dataset. (2) The shift met in use resembles the shift of the benchmark; here every dataset has the same protocol (prevalences uniform on the simplex, bags of $1000$ instances), so leaving one dataset out cannot test it, and a $\tau$ tuned on this protocol may not suit a stronger, weaker or different shift. The only fully label-free choice is the one on validation bags drawn from a part of $V$ that the method does not see (E5.3). The whole dataset is held out, not one pair, because its five pairs share $V$ and its test pool; holding out one pair would let the other four pairs of the same dataset, with the same error from $V$, choose its $\tau$.

**The test.** For each dataset, let $\bar W_d(1)$ and $\bar W_d(\tau_{-d})$ be the means over its five pairs of the per-pair mean Winkler scores. The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test ranks the $15$ differences $\bar W_d(1)-\bar W_d(\tau_{-d})$ by their absolute value and compares the sum of the ranks of the positive differences with what it would be if positive and negative signs were equally likely; a small $p$ means that the leave-one-dataset-out $\tau$ lowers the score more often and by more than chance would give. The datasets are the units because their errors from $V$ are independent, while those of the pairs of one dataset are not.

**Why validation bags cannot choose $\tau$ here.** Both methods use the rates of $h$ measured on $V$ (BayCAP-KDEy through $\hat C$, BayCAP-Hf through the counts of $V$). A bag drawn from $V$ has, on average over its draws, recalls equal to those of $V$, so the recall term of T.4 is zero on it: the error from $V$, which is what tempering should cover, does not occur on validation bags, and a selection on them would favour a $\tau$ that is too small. This is why the selection on validation bags (E5.3) needs a part of $V$ that the method does not see, and §T.9 shows what such bags can and cannot see.

## T.9 What calibration bags drawn from half of $V$ can see

**Setting.** Take one class $\omega_j$ and the recall $d$ of $h$ on it, on the test distribution. $V$ has $n$ instances of the class, split into two halves of $n/2$ each, $V_{\text{fit}}$ and $V_{\text{cal}}$. Write the errors of the recall estimated on each half and on the test pool as

$$
a=\hat d^{\text{fit}}-d,\qquad b=\hat d^{\text{cal}}-d,\qquad c=d^{\text{pool}}-d .
$$

Each estimate is a proportion of instances classified correctly, so its variance is $d(1-d)$ divided by the number of instances (the variance of the mean of independent indicators). With $\sigma_V^2=d(1-d)/n$, the variance on the full $V$, each half has $\operatorname{Var}(a)=\operatorname{Var}(b)=2\sigma_V^2$, and the pool of $n_p$ instances has $\operatorname{Var}(c)=\sigma_p^2=d(1-d)/n_p$. The three are computed on disjoint instances, so they are independent.

**The error on a calibration bag.** The method fitted on $V_{\text{fit}}$ uses the recall $\hat d^{\text{fit}}$; a calibration bag drawn from $V_{\text{cal}}$ has, on average over its draws, the recall $\hat d^{\text{cal}}$. Its recall error is therefore $\hat d^{\text{fit}}-\hat d^{\text{cal}}=a-b$, with variance $\operatorname{Var}(a)+\operatorname{Var}(b)=4\sigma_V^2$ (the variance of a difference of independent variables is the sum of their variances).

**The error of the deployed method.** The method of E5.3 is fitted on the full $V$, whose recall is the proportion correct over both halves; with halves of equal size, that is the average of the two, $\hat d^{\text{full}}=(\hat d^{\text{fit}}+\hat d^{\text{cal}})/2$, so its error is $(a+b)/2$. On a test bag, which has on average the recall of the pool, the recall error is $(a+b)/2-c$, with variance $\tfrac14(2\sigma_V^2+2\sigma_V^2)+\sigma_p^2=\sigma_V^2+\sigma_p^2$.

**The two are uncorrelated.** The covariance is bilinear, and the covariance of independent variables is $0$, so

$$
\operatorname{Cov}\!\Big(a-b,\ \frac{a+b}{2}-c\Big)=\frac12\operatorname{Var}(a)-\frac12\operatorname{Var}(b)+\frac12\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)-\frac12\operatorname{Cov}(b,a)-\operatorname{Cov}(a-b,c)=\frac12\cdot2\sigma_V^2-\frac12\cdot2\sigma_V^2=0 .
$$

*Intuition:* the difference between the two halves says nothing about their average. If the full $V$ happens to give a recall that is too high, as on `mammographic`, both halves tend to be too high together, and their difference does not show it. So the calibration bags cannot detect the actual displacement of a pair.

**What they do see.** The size of $a-b$ is set by $\sigma_V$, which is large when the class has few validation instances or a recall far from $0$ and $1$; summed over the classes as in §T.4, it is the typical size of the recall term of the pair. Its standard deviation, $2\sigma_V$, is twice that of the deployed method, $\sigma_V$ (when the pool is large, $\sigma_p\approx0$), so a selection that makes the calibration intervals cover will tend to choose a larger $\tau$ than the deployed method needs. A bias of the method that does not depend on the particular instances of $V$ (for example a bias of the quantifier under the shift) appears on the calibration bags as on the test bags, and the calibration can see it.

**The expected error from $V$.** For the diagnostic of E5.3, the recall term of the deployed method is $\sum_j\pi^Q_j(\hat d^{\text{full}}_j-d_j)$ (§T.4). Its errors in different classes are independent, so, for a given $\pi^Q$, its variance is $\sum_j(\pi^Q_j)^2\,d_j(1-d_j)/n^s_j$ (the variance of a sum of independent terms, with $\operatorname{Var}(xY)=x^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)$). With $\pi^Q$ uniform on the simplex, $\mathbb E[(\pi^Q_j)^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\pi^Q_j)+\mathbb E[\pi^Q_j]^2=\frac{K-1}{K^2(K+1)}+\frac1{K^2}=\frac{2}{K(K+1)}$, from the mean $1/K$ and the variance of a component of a $\operatorname{Dir}(\mathbf 1)$ (§T.2). Averaging over $\pi^Q$ and plugging in the recalls measured on $V$ gives

$$
\hat\sigma_{e_2}^2=\frac{2}{K(K+1)}\sum_j\frac{\hat d_j(1-\hat d_j)}{n^s_j} .
$$

For $K=2$ the factor is $1/3$, as in the check of `mammographic` in [[ext3_posterior_propagation|Extension 3]] E3.1. E5.3 expects $\tau_{\text{cal}}$ to grow with $\hat\sigma_{e_2}$ relative to the width of the interval.

## T.10 A concentrated and a sparse prior are not equally strong

E5.4 compares two symmetric Dirichlet distributions, $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf 1)$ with $\alpha=\lambda=3$ (concentrated) and $\alpha=\beta=1/3$ (sparse). Both have mean $\mathbf 1/K$: the mean of component $k$ of a $\operatorname{Dir}(a)$ is $a_k/\sum_la_l=\alpha/(\alpha K)=1/K$. They differ in two ways that matter for E5.4.

**Spread.** With the variance of §T.2, $\operatorname{Var}(\pi_k)=\bar a_k(1-\bar a_k)/(\sum_la_l+1)$, here

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\pi_k)=\frac{\frac1K\left(1-\frac1K\right)}{\alpha K+1},\qquad\frac{\operatorname{Var}_\alpha(\pi_k)}{\operatorname{Var}_1(\pi_k)}=\frac{K+1}{\alpha K+1},
$$

where the ratio compares with the uniform prior ($\alpha=1$) and the numerators cancel because the mean is the same. With $\alpha=3$ the ratio is $3/7\approx0.43$ at $K=2$ and $27/79\approx0.34$ at $K=26$, tending to $1/3$; with $\alpha=1/3$ it is $3/(5/3)=1.8$ at $K=2$ and $27/(26/3+1)\approx2.8$ at $K=26$, tending to $3$. For large $K$ the two are symmetric around the uniform prior, one three times less spread and one three times more; at $K=2$ they cannot be, since no Dirichlet has a variance larger than $K+1=3$ times that of the uniform one (the ratio grows to $K+1$ as $\alpha\to0$).

**Strength.** §T.2 reads a Dirichlet prior as $c=\sum_ka_k$ imaginary instances, here $c=\alpha K$: $3K$ for the concentrated prior, $K/3$ for the sparse one, $K$ for the uniform one. With a perfect and known classifier and $\tau=1$, the posterior is $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf 1+n)$ and its mean is

$$
\mathbb E[\pi^Q_k\mid U]=\frac{\alpha+n_k}{\alpha K+N}=w\,\frac1K+(1-w)\,\frac{n_k}{N},\qquad w=\frac{\alpha K}{\alpha K+N},
$$

by the same rewriting as in §T.2. The concentrated prior pulls each prevalence towards $1/K$ with a weight three times that of the uniform prior; the sparse prior pulls with a weight three times smaller. So the two informative priors of E5.4 are not equally strong: being wrong with the concentrated one moves the posterior far more than being wrong with the sparse one.

**Where the sparse prior acts.** Its weight is small, so it changes the posterior mostly where the data say little, i.e. on classes with few or no instances in the bag. For a class with $n_k=0$, the posterior mean above is $\alpha/(\alpha K+N)$. At $K=26$ and $N=100$ this is $3/178\approx0.017$ with the concentrated prior, $1/126\approx0.008$ with the uniform one and $(1/3)/(26/3+100)\approx0.003$ with the sparse one. If $m$ classes are absent from the bag, the posterior moves $m$ times these amounts of prevalence onto them, taking it from the classes that are present: with $m=13$, about $0.22$, $0.10$ and $0.04$. Bags drawn from $G_e$ have many absent classes when $K$ is large, so under $G_e$ the concentrated prior moves much more prevalence onto the absent classes than the uniform one does: this is the expected large cost of the ill-informed prior. Under $G_c$ few classes are absent and the sparse prior differs from the uniform one by less than one imaginary instance per class, so the expected cost of the ill-informed prior is small.

**From the prevalence to the accuracy.** The accuracy is close to $\sum_k\pi^Q_kd_k$ (§T.4), so moving a share $s$ of prevalence from the present classes to the absent ones changes it by about $s$ times the difference between the mean recall of the absent classes and that of the present ones. When the recalls are similar across classes, as with many classes and a good classifier, even a large move changes the accuracy little: this is the hypothesis offered in the Analysis of E5.1 for the missing gain at $K\ge8$, and E5.4 can show it again for macro-F1 and macro-K, which weigh classes differently.

**The sampler.** With $\beta<1$ the prior density grows without bound at the faces of the simplex, and the bags drawn from $G_e$ have true prevalences close to the faces. As in E5.1, where some parameters $3K\mu^\star_k$ were also below $1$, NUTS may report more divergent transitions, mostly for BayCAP-Hf; the pilot checks it.

## T.11 A prior on the right side, weaker than the distribution of the bags

E5.5 draws the target prevalences from $G=\operatorname{Dir}(\gamma\mathbf 1)$ with $\gamma=10$ or $\gamma=\tfrac1{10}$, and analyses them with a prior $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf 1)$ with $\alpha\in\{1,3,\tfrac13\}$, never equal to $\gamma$. This section shows that, in a simplified model, the prior with $\alpha$ between $1$ and $\gamma$ is better than the uniform one on average, and the prior on the other side of $1$ is worse.

**The model.** As in §T.2 and §T.10, the posterior mean of a prevalence is a weighted average of the centre $1/K$ of the prior and of what the bag says. Write $\hat p_k$ for the estimate of $\pi_k$ from the bag alone, and assume that it is unbiased with the variance of a proportion over $N_{\text{eff}}$ labelled instances (§T.2):

$$
\mathbb E[\hat p_k\mid\pi]=\pi_k,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat p_k\mid\pi)=\frac{\pi_k(1-\pi_k)}{N_{\text{eff}}}.
$$

With a perfect classifier $N_{\text{eff}}=N$. The posterior mean with the prior $\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf 1)$ is then

$$
m_k=w\,\frac1K+(1-w)\,\hat p_k,\qquad w=\frac{\alpha K}{\alpha K+N_{\text{eff}}},
$$

and $w$ grows with $\alpha$: the uniform prior has weight $w_1$, the concentrated prior $w_3>w_1$, the sparse prior $w_{1/3}<w_1$.

**The error of the posterior mean.** Its error is $m_k-\pi_k=w\,(\tfrac1K-\pi_k)+(1-w)(\hat p_k-\pi_k)$, the sum of a pull towards the centre and of the noise of the bag. Squaring and taking the expectation over the bags (over $\pi\sim G$ and over $\hat p$ given $\pi$),

$$
\mathbb E[(m_k-\pi_k)^2]=w^2\,\mathbb E[(\tfrac1K-\pi_k)^2]+(1-w)^2\,\mathbb E[(\hat p_k-\pi_k)^2]+2w(1-w)\,\mathbb E[(\tfrac1K-\pi_k)(\hat p_k-\pi_k)].
$$

The last term is $0$: given $\pi$, the factor $\tfrac1K-\pi_k$ is fixed and $\mathbb E[\hat p_k-\pi_k\mid\pi]=0$, so the conditional expectation of the product is $0$, and so is its average over $\pi$ (law of total expectation). The first term is the variance of $\pi_k$ under $G$, since its mean is $1/K$; with $v=\tfrac1K(1-\tfrac1K)$ and the variance of §T.10, $\mathbb E[(\tfrac1K-\pi_k)^2]=v/(\gamma K+1)$. The second is the average of the conditional variance, $\mathbb E[\pi_k(1-\pi_k)]/N_{\text{eff}}$, and

$$
\mathbb E[\pi_k(1-\pi_k)]=\mathbb E[\pi_k]-\mathbb E[\pi_k^2]=\frac1K-\Big(\operatorname{Var}(\pi_k)+\frac1{K^2}\Big)=v-\frac{v}{\gamma K+1}=v\,\frac{\gamma K}{\gamma K+1},
$$

using $\mathbb E[\pi_k^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\pi_k)+\mathbb E[\pi_k]^2$. Putting the pieces together,

$$
\mathbb E[(m_k-\pi_k)^2]=\frac{v}{\gamma K+1}\Big(w^2+(1-w)^2\,\frac{\gamma K}{N_{\text{eff}}}\Big).
$$

**The best weight and the distance from it.** As a function of $w$ this is a parabola opening upwards. Setting its derivative $2w-2(1-w)\gamma K/N_{\text{eff}}$ to $0$ gives

$$
w^\star=\frac{\gamma K}{\gamma K+N_{\text{eff}}},
$$

the weight of the matched prior $\alpha=\gamma$: in this model the matched prior gives the smallest error, as §T.3 says in general. Completing the square, the excess over the minimum is proportional to the squared distance from $w^\star$:

$$
\mathbb E[(m_k-\pi_k)^2]-\min_w\mathbb E[(m_k-\pi_k)^2]=\frac{v}{\gamma K+1}\Big(1+\frac{\gamma K}{N_{\text{eff}}}\Big)(w-w^\star)^2 .
$$

So a prior is better than another exactly when its weight is closer to $w^\star$. Under $G_c$ ($\gamma=10$) the weights are ordered $w_{1/3}<w_1<w_3<w^\star$: the right-side prior $\operatorname{Dir}(3\cdot\mathbf 1)$ is closer to $w^\star$ than the uniform one, so it is better, however far it is from $\gamma$; the wrong-side prior is farther, so it is worse. Under $G_e$ ($\gamma=\tfrac1{10}$) the order is reversed, $w^\star<w_{1/3}<w_1<w_3$, with the same conclusion. The intuition: the uniform prior already pulls the wrong amount, and a prior on the right side corrects part of that error, while one on the wrong side adds to it.

**How large.** With $N_{\text{eff}}=N=1000$, the ratio of the error of each prior to that of the uniform prior (evaluating the formula above) is, under $G_c$: $0.994$ (right side), $1.002$ (wrong side) and $0.984$ (matched) at $K=2$; $0.925$, $1.032$ and $0.833$ at $K=26$. Under $G_e$: $0.985$, $1.148$ and $0.984$ at $K=2$; $0.845$, $2.401$ and $0.833$ at $K=26$. So on prevalence the right-side prior recovers most of the gain of the matched prior under $G_e$ and about half of it under $G_c$; the gains are small at $K=2$ and grow with $K$, since the prior weight grows with $K$; and the cost of the wrong-side prior is large only under $G_e$, as in E5.4. With $N_{\text{eff}}<N$ all the weights grow and the effects are larger. These are errors on $\pi^Q$: E5.4 found that the accuracy follows them only where the recalls differ across classes (§T.10), so on vanilla accuracy the large-$K$ effects are expected to shrink, while on macro-F1 and macro-K they should show.

**The SQS methods.** SQS-H, SQS-Hbs and SQS-S take no prior on $\pi^Q$, so their intervals do not depend on the information. Their quantifiers are point estimators (PACC for SQS-H and SQS-Hbs, PACC with an MLP for SQS-S), clipped to the simplex: under $G_e$ the target prevalences lie near its faces, where the clipping biases the estimates and SQS-S finds fewer validation bags close to the target ([[ext6_sqs_s_design|Extension 6]], E6.1). SQS-B uses the posterior of BayesKDEy, the same as BayCAP-KDEy, and the prior enters it as in BayCAP-KDEy; it is expected to move with the information as BayCAP-KDEy does.

**The sampler.** The draws of $G_e$ lie even closer to the faces than in E5.4; E5.4 found that the sparse prior makes BayCAP-KDEy diverge on up to $22\%$ of the sparse bags, and SQS-B samples the same posterior, so both may diverge more.
